Five members of the public received the Smart Investor Awards for reporting boiler rooms and Internet scams at a presentation ceremony held yesterday at the SFC. (Note 1)

Their reports have resulted in the names of five dubious entities being added on the Alert List of unlicensed overseas companies, suspected scam websites and phishing scams on the SFC website. (Notes 2 and 3)

In two cases, an investor and an SFC licensee were cold-called by suspect fraudsters, claiming to operate from the United States and Antigua respectively, who lured them to invest in stocks traded in the United States. The investor approached the SFC and, upon the SFC’s advice, did not accept the offer and made no payment to the fraudster. The SFC licensee even managed to record his tele-conversations with the phoney brokers. The tape recordings show how boiler room operators used high pressure and seemingly professional sales tactics to urge potential investors to buy stocks that they recommended.

The other three cases are related to a new type of online scam called "phishing". The award winners all received emails supposedly from a major bank in Hong Kong, with which they in fact maintain electronic banking accounts, asking them to click on a link attached in the email to verify their account information. They were told they might no longer access their electronic banking accounts if they failed to do so. Doubtful of the authenticity of the emails, they reported to the SFC. The SFC put the entities on the Alert List to caution the public and referred them to the Hong Kong Monetary Authority.

Mr Peter Au-Yang, SFC’s Executive Director and Chief Operating Officer who presented the Awards yesterday, said: "Fraudulent activities are unacceptable in our financial markets. The scope of such activities and the tactics used by the fraudsters proliferate with time. We are glad to see the support of the community in working with the SFC to combat them. It is also encouraging to know that all of our five award winners are smart enough to have avoided any monetary losses. As always, investors’ self-protection is the best and first-line of defence against frauds."

From January 2004, the SFC has received 75 complaints of financial scams involving reported losses by investors of $6.8 million. Three of the victims were Hong Kong investors who had suffered a total loss of approximately $220,000.

It is part of the SFC’s investor education role to inform the public of scams and swindles and how to avoid falling prey to them. Some investor tips to avoid phishing scams are:

  • Don’t submit any personal information or send money to an unknown third party before verifying the legitimacy of the email.
  • If you have any hesitation regarding emails with urgent requests for personal financial information, contact the organisation concerned using a telephone number you know to be genuine, or open a new internet browser session and type in the company's correct web address, to check if the message received is authentic.
  • Check your bank account and credit card statements regularly to ensure that all transactions are authorised and legitimate.


Investors may continue to submit reports to the SFC for consideration of the Smart Investor Awards.

Notes to Editor:

1. The Smart Investor Awards were introduced in February 2004 to encourage the public to report financial scams such as boiler rooms and Internet scams. Three awards have previously been given out. Each winner received an SFC corporate watch and a certificate of recognition. Details of the Awards are available on the SFC website under the section of "Advice to Investors" - "Investor Alert". Photographs taken during the ceremony yesterday (2 September 2004) are available under "Investor Alert" – "Smart Investor Awards - Presentation of Smart Investor Awards".

2. The SFC first introduced the Alert List in November 2000 when boiler room activities became active. Investors have found the information useful. There are over 180 entities on the List now.

3. "Phishing" refers to fraudsters imitating reputable financial institutions (e.g. banks) and sending emails to entice their customers to disclose their credit card or bank account numbers and passwords/Personal Identification Numbers (PINs). They may use the information to swindle investors by running up bills or transferring money out of their bank accounts. Fraudsters normally send emails randomly to Internet users whose Internet domain information is publicly available, and hope that clients of the financial institutions that the fraudsters imitate will respond upon receipt of the emails.
