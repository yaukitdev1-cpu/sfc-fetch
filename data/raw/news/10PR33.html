Warrants may be a type of geared investment products derived from stocks but the prices of some warrants do not always move in tandem with the underlying stocks.

In his latest column, “Why Sometimes Warrant Prices Don’t Track Underlying Prices,” Dr Wise explains to investors some of the factors accounting for the divergence, including:

  • Changes in the demand and supply of the warrant – Supply and demand often have a considerable impact on the warrant’s price. Further issues of the same warrant, for example, will increase the supply of the warrant, possibly putting downward pressure on the price of a warrant which was previously in short supply.
  • Changes in implied volatility – Warrant issuers may have divergent views on the underlying and may therefore use different volatilities to price their warrants. Hence, changes in issuers’ expectation of the future volatility of an underlying may affect the warrant price.
  • Direct correlation between the warrant’s life span and its time value – Due to their limited lives, time value of warrants will drop as they approach expiry. In addition, short-dated deep out-of-the-money warrants usually are less sensitive to normal changes in the underlying as these warrants are unlikely to become in-the-money on expiry.


“The pricing of derivative warrants reflects the intricate workings of a number of factors,” Dr Wise says. “Do ask yourself first if you are prepared to accept the downside risk of losing your investment before trading in warrants.”

Dr Wise also reminds investors that liquidity providers are not obligated to support warrant prices; they only need to fulfil their liquidity provision obligations as stated in the listing documents. Investors should understand in advance how a warrant’s liquidity provider works and what its obligations are.
