Circular to Licensed Corporations and Associated Entities
Anti-Money Laundering / Combating Terrorist Financing
Identification and Reporting of Suspicious Transactions
Money laundering (“ML”) and terrorist financing (“TF”) have become global concerns
affecting all major financial centres. The highly international nature of the securities and
futures industry makes it vulnerable to ML and TF. This circular is intended to highlight
some of the key indicators of ML/TF activities and to remind licensed corporations (“LCs”)
and associated entities (“AEs”) to be vigilant in identifying and reporting suspicious
transactions.
Money laundering through the securities and futures sector
Illegal funds laundered through the securities and futures sector may be generated by illegal
activities both from outside and from within the sector (e.g. insider trading, market
manipulation and embezzlement). Since ML and TF are evolving in nature, LCs and AEs
need to be alert to various money laundering methods and tactics. The Financial Action Task
Force on Money Laundering (“FATF”)’s annual reports and reports on money laundering
typologies1 and the quarterly analysis reports issued by the Joint Financial Intelligence Unit
(“JFIU”)2 are some of the best sources of information on this subject.
Some key indicators of ML/TF activities include the following:
(a) Unrealistic wealth compared to client profile
A number of suspicious cases indicated that clients with little or no wealth and no
apparent employment had received a large amount of transfer deposits and been
engaged in a substantial amount of transactions.

(b) Unusual pattern of transactions
In some cases where clients were suspected to be manipulating the share prices, it was
noted that these clients had only traded heavily in one or two shares within a short
period of time, causing the price and trading volume of the shares to soar during that
period. After that, the accounts became dormant.
(c) Unusual settlement where cheques were issued to third parties with no apparent
reasons
Money launders may try to “layer” illegal funds by switching the funds between
several accounts. In some suspicious cases, it was noted that the clients requested the
brokers to issue third-party cheques and pay their sale proceeds to another person
(which may be an offshore company) with no apparent reasons.
(d) Defensive stance to questioning
Inexperienced launders may not have prepared a reasonable story concerning the
origin of their illicit funds or unlawful activities in their accounts. They may tend to
be defensive when questions are asked and are reluctant, without legitimate reasons,
to provide any information in response to the brokers’ questions.
Prevention of money laundering and terrorist financing
LCs and AEs should take appropriate measures to ensure that they do not become involved in
any types of ML or TF by accident, or through the possible collusion of staff. The
fundamental counter-measure is to strictly apply the “know your client” principles. This
means LCs and AEs should obtain satisfactory evidence to establish the identity of their
clients and have a good understanding of what is the normal and reasonable level and type of
activity for a particular client. LCs and AEs should not assume that the client due diligence
obligations have already been performed by the financial institutions that handle the initial
cash deposits. They need to conduct their own client due diligence.
LCs and AEs are reminded that each individual has the legal and regulatory obligations to
report suspicious transactions to the JFIU. In order to satisfy such obligations, they should
have systems to enable their staff to detect patterns of unusual or suspicious activity and
report them to their compliance officers. LCs and AEs should educate and train their staff
regularly so as to create and maintain their awareness and to remind them of their obligations
to report suspicious transactions.

LCs and AEs are also encouraged to commission regular reviews to check compliance with
the SFC’s revised Guidance Note on Prevention of Money Laundering and Terrorist
Financing (“Guidance Note”) which came into effect on 30 April 2006. Upon request, they
should be able to demonstrate to the SFC that their anti-money laundering policies and
procedures are in compliance with the revised Guidance Note.
Consequences for failure to report suspicious transactions
The JFIU’s reports indicated that the number of suspicious transactions reported by the
securities and futures sector remains low as compared with other financial sectors. LCs and
AEs are reminded to be vigilant to detect accounts and transactions which may be related to
possible ML and TF activities and report any suspicious transactions to the JFIU. They
should also bear in mind the serious consequences that can arise from their failure to combat
ML and TF:
(a) individual members of management and staff may be open to criminal prosecution
and subject to fine and imprisonment;
(b) failure to comply with the requirements of the Guidance Note will, in the absence of
extenuating circumstances, reflect adversely on the fitness and properness of a LC/AE
and the SFC may take disciplinary actions against the LC/AE; and
(c) the reputation of the LC/AE and of Hong Kong may be damaged and the costs of
reputation repair and recovery could be enormous, far outweighing the costs of
precaution.
