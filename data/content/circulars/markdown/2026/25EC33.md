Circular to licensed corporations
Prevention and handling of unauthorised trading incidents
1.

The Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) has noted an increase in the number
of unauthorised trading incidents at licensed corporations (LCs), which have resulted
in financial losses to their clients in some cases. This circular sets out the SFC's
regulatory expectations in relation to the prevention and handling of unauthorised
trading incidents in LCs’ client accounts.

2.

The root causes of and the way that unauthorised trading incidents are perpetrated
may differ from case to case. However, in the recent spate of such incidents, it is
suspected that the perpetrators executed a man-in-the-middle attack. By sending
short message service (SMS) messages with embedded phishing hyperlinks to
brokers’ clients to trick them into entering their user credentials into fake websites
which closely resembled LCs’ genuine websites, the perpetrators were then able to
intercept clients’ usernames, login passwords and data used to authenticate the
clients as part of the LCs’ two-factor authentication (2FA) process, including SMS
one-time passwords (OTPs) and biometric data, to access clients’ accounts and
conduct the unauthorised trading.

3.

In light of the above, the SFC expects LCs to take appropriate measures in the
following areas to prevent and handle unauthorised trading incidents:
(a)

Signing up for the SMS Sender Registration Scheme;

(b)

Raising client awareness; and

(c)

Enhancing procedures and controls for identifying unauthorised access and
transactions in client accounts.

(A)

Signing up for the SMS Sender Registration Scheme

4.

The free SMS Sender Registration Scheme (Scheme) administered by the Office of
the Communications Authority (OFCA) 1 enables registered participants to send SMS
messages with the prefix “#” to help recipients verify the identity of the sender of the
SMS messages and prevent fraudsters from impersonating the sender. Accordingly,
an LC should, without undue delay, sign up for the Scheme and make the necessary
arrangements with telecommunications service providers to enable it to send SMS
messages with the prefix “#”.

5.

After successfully registering for the Scheme, an LC should (i) only send SMS
messages to clients with the prefix “#”; and (ii) inform clients via its usual
communication channels that:

(B)

Raising client awareness

6.

LCs should from time to time raise their clients’ awareness of the dangers of clicking
on embedded hyperlinks in SMS messages purportedly sent by LCs and how this
may result in unauthorised trading in their accounts. Apart from sending reminders to
clients periodically about the points in paragraph 5 above, an LC should also step up
client outreach and engagement efforts, particularly if the LC has encountered
unauthorised trading incidents or is on notice that such incidents are occurring within
the industry.

7.

Accordingly, an LC should:
(a)

Put on its website and mobile application prominent warnings about
unauthorised trading incidents and reminders on how clients can protect
themselves 4, such as by not clicking on embedded hyperlinks in SMS
messages and not entering confidential personal information, user credentials
and OTPs into websites or mobile applications that they are re-directed to;

(b)

Remind clients to regularly check for any unusual activities in relation to their
accounts, such as by reviewing the notifications sent to them on system login,
password reset, trade execution or changes to client and account related
information, to identify signs of attackers’ attempt to conduct unauthorised
trading, whether successful or not;

(c)

Where clients have chosen to opt out of receiving trade execution or system
login notifications, remind clients of the associated risks regularly (at least
annually) and inform them of the option to receive such notifications as well as
the process to enable this;

(C)

Enhancing procedures and controls for identifying unauthorised access and
transactions in client accounts

8.

LCs should implement an effective monitoring and surveillance mechanism to detect
unauthorised access to its clients’ accounts 11. The tools and mechanisms adopted by
LCs should be commensurate with their business size and complexity 12.

9.

LCs should be vigilant in identifying red flags which may indicate unauthorised access
to or trading in their clients’ accounts. Potential red flags include:
(a)

A sudden change in the volume, pattern or type of transactions being
conducted in the client account which is not commensurate with the LC’s
knowledge of the client based on its KYC records or the client’s past
transaction history. For example, a client who mainly invested in blue-chip
stocks suddenly shifted his portfolio to small-cap stocks or a previously
inactive account suddenly engaging in frequent trading of small-cap stocks;

(b)

A number of clients trading in small-cap or illiquid stocks and their trades
representing a sizeable portion of the daily turnover;

(c)

The IP address used to login to the client account changing from one location
to another in a short period of time;

10.

LCs are expected to have internal control procedures and operational capabilities
which can be reasonably expected to protect its operations and clients from financial
loss arising from theft, fraud and other dishonest acts 13. In addition to the measures in
sections (A) and (B) of this circular, LCs should enhance their existing policies,
procedures and internal controls to better protect their clients’ accounts against
unauthorised trading and mitigate its effects.

Take immediate steps to prevent any further unusual activities from

(b)

Where it knows or suspects that unauthorised trading might have taken place

crime, promptly report its knowledge or suspicion to the Joint Financial
Intelligence Unit (JFIU) by filing a suspicious transaction report (STR) 15 so that
the relevant authorities may take timely follow-up actions. For the avoidance
of doubt, the counterparty LC on the other side of the (suspected)
unauthorised trade also has an obligation to file an STR to the JFIU if it is on
notice of the suspected crime;

(c)

Report to the SFC immediately upon any material failure, error or defect in the
operation or functioning of its systems or identification of suspicious
transactions as required under paragraph 12.5 of the Code of Conduct; and

(d)

Keep abreast of the latest cybersecurity threat landscape by subscribing for
cyber threat intelligence from different credible sources, such as Scameter,
and share its cyber threat intelligence (such as by reporting threats to
Scameter+ and the HKPF).

Consider hardening the cybersecurity defences of its website against man-inthe-middle phishing attacks (given that websites may be more susceptible
than mobile applications to such attacks) by blocking connection attempts

(b)

Given the security concerns associated with SMS OTPs, consider adopting
more robust client authentication methods or implementing compensating
controls as appropriate;

(c)

Consider displaying pop-up messages 16 to clients when they go through the
LC’s 2FA process, to warn them against proceeding with the authentication
process if it was not initiated by them;

(d)

Where there is an attempt to register a new login device for a client account,
prior to implementing the request, aside from requiring the client to go through
the LC’s 2FA process, consider notifying the client about the request via a
channel different from the one used to submit the request; such notification to
the client should include a reminder to the client to not approve the request if it
was not originated by the client;

(e)

When a newly registered login device is used to access a client’s account,
consider verifying with the client whether the new login device genuinely
belongs to the client before allowing transactions to be placed with this device;

(f)

Consider requiring clients to verify their identity again prior to executing
trades, such as by implementing 2FA or requiring a password specifically for
trade executions;

(g)

Consider disallowing clients from having concurrent logins to its trading
platform; and

(h)

Consider regularly and proactively searching for fake or fraudulent websites
and mobile applications which impersonate the LC with a view to having such
websites and mobile applications taken down by the domain service provider
and app store respectively, as well as warning its clients against such
websites and mobile applications.
