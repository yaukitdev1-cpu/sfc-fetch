To SFC’s Licensed Corporations

Advice to Investors on Exotic Warrants
The SFC issued a press release on 26 June 2003 to give investors general advice on exotic
warrants. We write now to remind licensed corporations that, pursuant to the requirements
under the Code of Conduct for Persons Licensed by or Registered with the Commission,
licensed corporations dealing with clients who trade in exotic warrants should ensure these
products are suitable for your clients and assure yourselves that your clients understand the
nature and risks of these products. For more details, you should refer to the Code of Conduct
which is available on the SFC’s website www.hksfc.org.hk.
The SFC has recently received a number of complaints over the performance of exotic
warrants and most of these complaints seem to have arisen from a lack of understanding of
the product features, and the risk and return profile of exotic warrants. For instance, some
investors were not aware that a “capped call warrant” imposes a limit on the upside profit
potential and complained that the warrant price remained stagnant even though the
underlying stock price had moved up. Other investors confused a “locked-in return warrant”
with a standard warrant and alleged that it underperformed the standard warrant when the
price of the underlying stock surged. It turns out that the locked-in return warrant was out of
the money at past fixing dates and, therefore, it had already lost some of its value.
We have also noticed that some investors do not fully understand the role of liquidity
providers. Although issuers bear the responsibility of providing liquidity in their warrants via
liquidity providers, this does not imply that they are obliged to take up all the outstanding
orders in the market. Issuers provide quotes via liquidity providers at the price level they
deem fair and within the maximum spread to which they have committed in the respective
listing document. Therefore, there is a chance that their quotes may not match investors’
orders or expected price levels.
In the above press release, investors were reminded to read the listing documents for exotic
warrants and understand the terms and conditions. This is to ensure that they are aware of
each issue’s unique features and understand how it works before making an investment
decision. Investors were also advised to seek professional financial advice if they have any
doubt.

Exotic Warrants
When you are checking on warrant prices on the Teletext, have you ever noticed that some
derivative warrants carry a symbol “X” in their names? Do you know what that symbol
means?
The symbol “X” refers to a special class of derivative warrants called “exotic warrants”. As
the name implies, exotic warrants are derivative warrants with exotic features that are
different from standard call or put warrants. Some of them are modified single call or put
warrants, while others are effectively a combination of two or more standard warrants.
How do exotic warrants work? What should I be aware of?
Make sure you know the salient features of an exotic warrant and understand how it works:
Each type of exotic warrant carries unique features and behaves quite differently from
standard warrants and other exotic warrants in its response to the price levels or price
movements of the underlying asset. Here, we give some examples.
Capped warrants: These limit or “cap” your potential profit but are generally cheaper than
standard warrants which give you unlimited upside profit potential. They can be call or put
warrants and are sometimes known as call spread warrants and put spread warrants. Suppose
you buy a capped call warrant at $2, on underlying stock A with an exercise price of $40 and
a cap level of $48. If the price of stock A is above $42 (the breakeven point) on the expiry
date, you can make a potential profit as illustrated in Diagram A. Generally, the higher the
stock price, the higher your return. However, unlike a standard call warrant which has
unlimited upside profit potential, your maximum potential profit is “capped” at $6 even if the
stock price rises beyond $48.

An investor who buys the above capped call warrant is effectively buying a call warrant at an
exercise price of $40 and selling a call warrant at a higher exercise price of $48. The
premium from the warrant “sold” makes the capped call warrant cheaper than a standard call
warrant.
Locked-in return warrants: These are usually warrants with longer term (e.g. 18 months)
maturity at launch. They take snapshots on regular (e.g. quarterly) fixing dates and will
“lock-in” the profits if the warrants are in the money on those fixing dates. The average of

Digital warrants: Payoff at expiry for standard warrants varies at different levels of the
underlying price. On the other hand, digital warrants pay you either a “fixed” amount of cash
or nothing, depending only on whether the warrant is in the money at expiry. Suppose you
buy a digital call warrant at $1, on underlying stock B with an exercise price of $30 and a
potential payout of $5. If the price of stock B is at or above $30 on the expiry date, you can
make a “fixed” potential profit of $4 as illustrated in Diagram B. If the stock price is below
$30, you will lose all your initial outlay. Also, if the stock price is at $50, you still only make
$4, as the warrant price will not rise as much as a non-digital warrant as the stock price rises.

Dividend accumulator warrants: These are usually warrants with longer term (e.g. 18
months) maturity at launch. They pay you dividends similar to the ordinary cash dividends
paid on the underlying share during the lives of the warrants prior to their expiry dates.
To know more about the features of an exotic warrant, get a copy of its listing document from
the HKEx website. It sets out all the terms and conditions of the warrant. Some warrant
issuers also provide listing documents, term sheets or other marketing materials on their own
websites for public access.
Understand the pros and cons of an exotic warrant to see whether it meets your investment
needs: Dealing in warrants involves risks that may be different from investing in the
underlying securities. Like standard warrants, exotic warrants offer a leveraged exposure to
movements in their underlying assets. Some exotic warrants offer more leverage than
standard warrants while others offer less. Remember, leverage works both ways. Although
higher leverage may give you a higher return, it also exposes you to a higher risk.
Besides, each type of exotic warrant has its own advantages and disadvantages. For example,
a capped call warrant is normally cheaper than a standard call warrant with the same exercise
price and expiry date. This is because you forgo any profit potential above the cap level (see
Diagram A). While a dividend accumulator warrant lets you enjoy dividends paid on the
underlying share, it certainly costs more than a standard warrant. A digital call warrant will
outperform a standard warrant if the underlying price is at or within a certain range above the

Ensure you know the differences between an exotic warrant and a standard warrant:
Because of the difference in its payoff structure, the change in the warrant price of an exotic
warrant in response to movements of the underlying asset is also different from that of a
standard warrant. Make sure you fully understand the risk and return profile of an exotic
warrant before making any investment decisions. Do not expect it to behave in the same way
as a similar standard warrant. If in doubt, seek professional financial advice.
Be aware of different symbols used by different newspapers for identifying exotic warrants:
Some newspapers use the symbol “X” to identify all types of exotic warrants in their pricing
page. Others adopt the symbol “X” to represent only one type of exotic warrant, say dividend
accumulators, and use other symbols to differentiate other exotic warrants. So, when you are
checking warrant information in newspapers, read the legend first and understand what the
symbols represent. Do not mix up different types of exotic warrants.
Are commonly used analytical tools like premium and gearing applicable to exotic
warrants?
They should be used and interpreted with care. Exotic warrants are sometimes a combination
of several warrants. A simple calculation using such analytical tools may sometimes give
misleading results. For example, the premium of a standard warrant is defined as the
percentage by which the warrant price exceeds its intrinsic value (i.e. spot price minus
exercise price). It can also be viewed as the time value of a warrant. If we use the same
formula to compute the premium of a capped call warrant, an anomaly of “negative”
premium may sometimes occur. Why?
This is because a capped call warrant effectively consists of buying a call warrant at the lower
exercise price (long call at the strike level) and selling another call warrant at the higher
exercise price (short call at the cap level). The value of the capped call warrant is the value
of the long call warrant minus the value of the short call warrant with each warrant being
valued at the sum of the intrinsic value and the time value. Therefore, the premium or the
time value of the capped warrant (Tc) is equal to the time value of the long call warrant minus
the time value of the short call warrant (Tl - T2). In the event that the time value of the long
call warrant is lower than that of the short call warrant, this may result in “negative”
premium.
Should I buy exotic warrants?
As in making other investments, you should consider carefully whether a particular exotic
warrant is suitable for you in light of your experience, investment objectives and financial
position.
Exotic warrants are designed for those who have a clear view and strategy on the movement
and level of the underlying asset. For instance, a capped call warrant is a cheaper tool for

Dividend accumulator warrants are more investment style instruments. They target investors

After all, exotic warrants are for experienced investors who are willing to spend the time to
understand the nature of the products so that they can choose the one which meet their market
view and investment objective. Do not buy a warrant simply because it looks cheaper than
the others, without considering why it is cheaper and whether it suits your needs.
