Chairman of the Securities and Futures Commission, Mr Andrew Sheng, today issues an open letter to investors in Hong Kong in the wake of concerns by some investors about the propriety of transactions proposed by the management of listed companies. The letter aims to explain the respective roles of the Commission and minority shareholders in relation to corporate transactions.

The letter is attached. Editors are welcome to publish it in full.

The main points are summarised as follows:

3. It is fundamental to the credibility and integrity of the Commission that it must not get involved in the financial or commercial merits or demerits of corporate transactions, including pricing; these are matters reserved for listed company management, shareholders and financial advisers.

- Secondly, regulators identify areas where these process rules can be improved.

*5. Regulators essentially operate as referees ensuring compliance with the rules of the game; we will not make judgements about which "side" is in the right about proposed transactions, or indeed which "side" should win.*

7. Management that breaches rules on fairness and transparency must be held accountable. The Commission will enforce these rules without fear or favour. There are more enforcement options under the new Securities and Futures Ordinance. The Commission last week also proposed rules designed to strengthen the disclosure based regime.

8. The Commission, the Stock Exchange and other public bodies in Hong Kong (such as the Standing Committee on Company Law Reform) are working together to introduce new rules, standards and laws designed to improve corporate governance.

9. We encourage investors to communicate with us and report any suspected breaches of securities laws, rules and regulations.

10. Lastly, a reminder to investors: Get the facts before you invest.

SFC Chairman Andrew Sheng's Letter to Investors


*"13 May 2002*

Dear Investors,

The Securities and Futures Commission has noted recent complaints made to the Commission directly or through letters in the press that question the propriety of transactions proposed by the management of listed companies, often claiming that they are to the disadvantage of the listed companies and their shareholders.

Of course, the Commission cannot comment on specific cases. The impartiality of the Commission would be called into question if the Commission were to do so.

However, it is important for the investing public to understand the Commission's role in relation to corporate transactions, and also the role of minority shareholders in such transactions.

Fundamentally, Hong Kong operates a disclosure-based regulatory system. All major markets in the world have adopted this approach. The merit of a transaction is not a matter for the regulators to determine. Our primary objective is to ensure that public investors receive reliable information disclosure in order to make the best judgement on transactions or events for themselves.

Role of the Commission

In Hong Kong, the front-line regulator in the listed sector is the Stock Exchange of Hong Kong, with specific regulatory functions being undertaken by the Commission.

The Commission investigates suspected breaches of securities laws, together with related rules and regulations. It administers and ensures compliance with the Codes on Takeovers and Mergers and Share Repurchases as well as aspects of prospectus law. It also approves changes to the Listing Rules. These functions are critical to the proper functioning of the market.

It is fundamental to the credibility and integrity of the Commission that it must not get involved in the financial or commercial merits or demerits of corporate transactions, including pricing; these are matters reserved for listed company management, shareholders and financial advisers. However, both the Commission and the Stock Exchange have a very powerful role to play in regulating the way in which management conducts transactions.

This role has two important aspects.

Firstly, the enforcement of "process" rules which ensure that shareholders have enough information on which to base their investment decisions (i.e. timely, accurate and full disclosure of information about a transaction) and, in suitable cases, to subject transactions to shareholder approval and other requirements (e.g. for independent advice to be given to minority shareholders). Both the Listing Rules of the Stock Exchange and the Commission's Takeovers and Repurchases Codes are founded on these principles of transparency and fairness.

Secondly, regulators identify areas where these process rules can be improved; reform is a continuing priority area both under the Listing Rules and the Codes. For example, the Takeovers and Repurchases Codes underwent a major overhaul this year and fundamental Listing Rules changes focused on corporate governance are now under consultation.

Provided that the rules, codes and all relevant regulations and laws are complied with, regulators will not attempt to intervene in any of the commercial aspects of corporate transactions. To do so would be quite wrong; commercial advantage or disadvantage is for the market to decide. Regulators essentially operate as referees ensuring compliance with the rules of the game; we will not make judgements about which "side" is in the right about proposed transactions, or indeed which "side" should win. To do otherwise would impair our impartiality, and would run counter to the way in which regulators must and do function in all developed markets.

Role of Investors

An investor buys or sells shares in a listed company based wholly or partly on judgements about the value of the company, as well as the level of corporate governance. We realise that shareholders may feel aggrieved if they believe that corporate management is failing them. But if this is purely an issue of competence or a history of bad decision making impacting on value, and the rules have not been violated, the ultimate practical remedy for investors may be to sell. We believe that this principle is well understood and accepted by investors in Hong Kong, as it is by investors elsewhere in the world.

This principle can however only operate properly if rules requiring full and fair disclosure of important developments are complied with. These rules are central to the operation of securities markets; it is clear that share price performance after an announced transaction would often reflect investors' views on the transaction and their confidence in the management. A fully informed market is probably the most important market good. Although commercial decisions are for management, it is the regulator's job to ensure that investors have all relevant information so that they can take a view and deal accordingly.

It follows that if management breaches rules which ensure fairness and transparency for investors, they must be held accountable. The market has the right to expect that we will enforce these rules without fear or favour. In line with our duty to ensure that our process regulations adapt to changing circumstances and new demands, our enforcement tools are also under continual review. The Legislative Council has made improvements to our enforcement options under the new Securities and Futures Ordinance. On Monday, 6 May, we announced proposals which are designed to enable the Commission to subject corporate disclosure documents to more rigorous investigation and additional sanctions in cases where disclosure is defective.

Corporate Governance

Although as a general principle regulators cannot get involved in the pros and cons of particular transactions from a commercial perspective, both the Commission, the Stock Exchange and other public bodies in Hong Kong (such as the Standing Committee on Company Law Reform) are continuing to devote considerable effort to the introduction of new rules, standards and laws designed to create an environment which leads to better corporate decision making. These initiatives fall under the umbrella of "corporate governance", and include comprehensive proposals by the Stock Exchange which are now under public consultation, as well as proposals for law change issued by the Standing Committee.

The goal is to ensure that corporate decision making procedures match international best practice.

We continue to encourage investors to communicate with us and, in particular, to report to us any suspected breaches of securities laws, rules and regulations.

Lastly, I would like to urge all investors again: Get the facts before you invest. The regulators' job is to ensure that investors are able to get all the facts by enforcing our disclosure rules.

Andrew Sheng
Chairman"
