Corporate governance investigations are one of the Securities and Futures Commission's key priorities for enforcement this year, according to Mr Alan Linning, Executive Director of Enforcement.

Mr Linning made this remark today at a media conference to explain the philosophy and processes of the SFC's enforcement work.

He said the enforcement objectives of the SFC were to protect investors and the public interest, as well as ensuring markets were fair, efficient and transparent.

Mr Linning said the SFC had prioritised three key areas for enforcement in 2002/2003. They are investigations into corporate misgovernance, serious misconduct by intermediaries involving dishonesty, and market misconduct such as market manipulation and insider dealing.

He said the SFC was "actively investigating" a number of significant cases that had been subject of press comments over the last few months. Details could not be given because of the obligation of secrecy imposed by section 59 of the Securities and Futures Commission Ordinance.

Mr Linning added: "The fact that an investigation is being conducted is market sensitive information which may result in a drop in a company's share price even if the investigation is subsequent curtailed with no reason for further action being taken. Inappropriate disclosure of the identity of the subject of investigation could therefore cause damage to shareholders."

On 6 May, the SFC consulted the market on proposals to require statutory filing of listing application documents and listed company announcements with the SFC. Mr Linning said this "dual key" approach would bring Hong Kong into line with international practice in providing for a statutory regulator of corporate information disclosure to the public.

He said the new Securities and Futures Ordinance (SFO), which is expected to become effective early next year, would provide a greater spectrum of sanctions to remedy disclosure failures, punish those responsible for them and deter others.

Under the SFO, the SFC's powers of investigation of listed company affairs will be enhanced. Its powers of production and inspection of documents and its ability to obtain statements will be extended to banks, auditors and "any other person" in connection with corporate misgovernance investigations.

Mr Linning said the SFC would also co-operate with other enforcement agencies in Hong Kong and overseas to ensure maximum and proper use of all of these new powers in order to enhance Hong Kong's international credibility.

Mr Linning said: "The Enforcement Division is committed to doing all it can to prevent and deter corporate misgovernance, serious misconduct by intermediaries and market misconduct."

However, he added that the market should not assume lesser offences would go unpunished.

Mr Linning said a fair, efficient and transparent market required timely guidance from its regulators on enforcement matters. "We will strive to complete our investigations in these areas quickly and publicise the results as soon as appropriate," he said.
