The SFC will this year refocus its disciplinary resources on the areas of corporate finance, fund managers and investment advisers, and banks.

A tougher stance will be taken on three key failings – dishonesty, conflicts of interests, and control-supervision failures that put investors at risk.

SFC’s Executive Director of Enforcement, Mr Alan Linning, said at a media briefing today that the SFC would get tougher on misconduct so there would be a greater deterrent effect.

The Securities and Futures Ordinance, which came into force on 1 April 2003, has provided for a wider range of powerful and proportionate sanctions against misconduct. The SFC may reprimand an entity, impose fines ($10 million or up to three times profit made/loss avoided), suspend or revoke a licence (both including for part of a business).

"The SFO heralds in a new era for SFC discipline," Mr Linning said.

As stated previously, the SFC’s enforcement priorities this year (which include criminal prosecution, market misconduct tribunal hearings, other court application powers and discipline) are:


listed company crime and misconduct:
o more listed company investigations and action with dual filing
o sponsor failings in discipline
o tougher disclosure of interests regime- market crimes with new civil and criminal options; and
- dishonest intermediaries or those who put client assets at risk

The SFC’s disciplinary priorities form an integral part of the overall enforcement priorities.

Mr Linning said: "The SFC licenses intermediaries to ensure that they’re fit and proper. We discipline licensees and their management who are dishonest, incompetent or financially unsound to protect investors and keep market confidence."

Last year, the SFC inquired into 163 licensees, finished 90 inquiries and formally punished 86 licensees, including 12 for serious misconduct which resulted in revocation or lengthy suspension of licences. Such conduct include theft, cheating clients, not paying debts, lying to the SFC, Takeovers Code breaches and helping market manipulators.

The combination of the new SFO powers and the changing business environment has enabled the SFC to refine its disciplinary priorities. It has decided on a two-focus approach: (1) a focus on industry sectors and (2) a focus on types of conduct.

On industry sectors, the SFC will continue to closely examine brokerage misconduct that puts investors at risk. In addition, the SFC will take a closer look at other market sectors, including banks’ capital markets departments, corporate finance advisers, funds managers and investment advisers. These are areas of new jurisdiction, areas where market concerns have recently been expressed or areas where investor participation is increasing (funds management and investment advice particularly).

The SFC will focus on the most serious types of misconduct: dishonesty, conflicts of interest and serious internal control and management failings that expose investors to actual or possible loss. Dishonesty includes theft, lying to the SFC or others, insider dealing, market manipulation; conflicts of interest cover all types of conflict, actual and perceived, and not just breaches of fiduciary duty; serious internal control and management failings cover all types of institutional failings that allow individual and corporate misconduct to occur, such as inadequate supervision of staff, poor internal systems to stop theft or trading malpractices or ensure fair treatment of clients, poor anti money laundering systems, inadequate compliance, etc.
