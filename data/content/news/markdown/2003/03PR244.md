The SFC has recently received a number of complaints on trading of commodity futures contracts and considers it appropriate to issue the following general investor advice.

Commodity futures contracts are futures contracts of which the underlying asset is a commodity such as copper, rubber, crude oil, corn, soybean and raw sugar. These products are currently traded in overseas markets such as the US and Japan.

Most of the complaints have involved inexperienced investors being recommended to trade in commodity futures, when they did not fully understand the product features, associated risks and transaction costs involved.

Before trading in commodity futures, investors are advised to note the following:


- Commodity futures are leveraged instruments. Investors are only required to make a margin deposit usually only a small fraction of the contract value. Any minor movement in the price of the underlying commodity translates into a very significant profit or loss compared to the initial margin deposit. Futures trading can bring substantial profits, but also substantial losses. Make sure you fully understand the product, contract specifications and risks.
- You may have to put up additional cash to meet margin calls if the market moves against you so that the margin deposit falls below the maintenance margin level. If you are not able to do so quickly, your brokerage may liquidate your position without your consent. You will remain liable for any resulting loss and you can lose more than your deposit.
- Read the client agreement and the risk disclosure statement carefully. A client agreement must clearly explain the services, brokerage commission, charges, margin procedures and risks of futures trading.
- Mind the transaction costs. A flat commission fee is usually charged each time a contract is bought or sold. While the flat commission might be only a small percentage of the underlying value, it might constitute a sizeable percentage of the deposit.
- Think carefully before giving discretion to your account executive. If you really want to rely on your account executive to make investment decisions, this authority must be given in writing, detailing the extent of the discretion together with a clear explanation of your investment objectives and risk profile. Regardless of what your account executive might say, a standard discretionary agreement is usually very wide.
- Beware of churning. To prevent your account executive from overtrading simply to earn more commission, you should review your contract notes and statements carefully to keep track of the account status.

To help investors learn more about commodity futures, the SFC has posted an article entitled *Commodity Futures Trading Is Not For Amateurs* on its website under the "Advice to Investors/Questions Corner" section.

Retail investors can call the SFC’s Investor Hotline at 2840 9333 for educational information on futures products, including commodity futures.
