The SFC today releases for public consultation proposed measures to strengthen the overall regulatory framework for brokerage firms involved in securities margin financing (SMF).

The proposed measures are a set of targeted reforms designed to significantly reduce the existing risks to investors and the market arising from excessive pooling and re-pledging of margin clients’ collateral and aggressive lending by SMF providers. 

The proposals are based on the recommendations made by the SFC’s Working Group on the Review of the Financial Regulatory Framework for Licensed Corporations (Working Group), whose report was presented to the Legislative Council Panel on Financial Affairs (FAP) in March 2004 (Note 1).  The FAP supported the SFC’s intention to consult the public on the recommended measures.

The C.A. Pacific incident is a good example of the danger of excessive pooling and re-pledging of client collateral. After the C.A. Pacific incident, the law was tightened to bring SMF activities within the regulatory framework administered by the SFC. However, the law still permits the pooling and re-pledging of client collateral including the collateral of those margin clients who have not borrowed.

In fact, other major financial markets prohibit any re-pledging of non-borrowing client collateral. However, there is currently no market wide infrastructure in Hong Kong to allow all brokerage firms to move to such a model immediately.

Currently, there are 243 firms providing securities margin financing in Hong Kong. Of these 243 SMF providers, 154 finance their margin lending business out of their own funds. They do not re-pledge client collateral. Although the other 89 firms do re-pledge client collateral, most of them operate in a prudent manner. Only a small number of SMF providers take advantage of the regulatory loophole by excessively re-pledging their client collateral and lending aggressively. These SMF providers are tempted to lend aggressively due to the lenient Securities and Futures (Financial Resources) Rules (FRR) haircut percentages on their client collateral.  If their SMF provider(s) fail, clients and investors may not get back their stocks.

The Consultation Paper proposes the following two main measures:

1. Introduce a **per-firm re-pledging limit** by capping the amount of client collateral that an SMF provider can re-pledge as security for its borrowings at a percentage of the aggregate margin loans. The lower the re-pledging limit, the greater the level of investor and systemic protection it would provide.

2. **Adjust the haircut percentages** under the FRR so that client collateral may be more realistically valued.  Brokers are still allowed to lend to their clients on more favourable terms but they will be required to put in their own capital to cover the added risk, rather than risking the securities of their clients. 

Our proposed measures are designed to encourage SMF providers to improve their pooling and margin lending practices. The proposed measures would not have any significant impact on the majority of SMF providers who honestly, responsibly and prudently operate their business. Only a small handful of SMF providers will actually need to put in further capital or find an alternative source of funding in order to comply with these two measures.  Basically, this small group includes those firms that re-pledge excessively and/or lend imprudently. 

Mrs Alexa Lam, SFC’s Executive Director of Intermediaries and Investment Products, said:  “The regulatory loophole must be plugged.  Excessive re-pledging and imprudent lending poses an unfair and unacceptable risk to investors and to the market at large. Hong Kong cannot afford to have another collapse like C.A. Pacific.”

“The brokerage industry has been doing well, recording a total net profit of almost $5 billion in the first half of 2004. This is perhaps the best time to deal with these issues, with the support of the public.”

The SFC is open minded to feedback and has no firm views on various key issues such as the appropriate level of re-pledging limit, implementation of transitional arrangements and other proposed measures etc. In addition, we will also consider relaxing certain existing FRR requirements (Note 2), depending on whether the final regulatory measures are able to adequately address the inherent risks in SMF.
