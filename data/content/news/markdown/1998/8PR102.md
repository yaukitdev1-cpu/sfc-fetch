**"Transparency, Information Technology, and Governance"**

(Speech presented by Mr Andrew Sheng, Chairman,
Securities and Futures Commission
at International Company Secretaries Conference
on 24 November 1998)

 


Ladies and Gentlemen, 

 

Introduction

I am very honoured to be invited today to say a few words on Corporate Governance. Since the whole Conference is on this subject, with papers by some of the leading experts of the world, I doubt very much I have very much new to add. Moreover, having been in the job of Chairman of the SFC for less than eight weeks, there is no way that I can give a definitive tome on Corporate Governance.

I thought what I would do this morning is to mix the subject with two issues that I recently did some work on: Transparency and Information Technology.

As you all know, one of the factors often held responsible for the Asian crisis is weak corporate governance, where corporations engaged in excessive over-leverage, aided by implicit state guarantees, lax banking supervision and what Professor Paul Krugman* called crony capitalism. Indeed, some would suggest that the Asian crisis has called into question the whole area of political governance, but that is a larger subject than what we are addressing today.

* Paul Krugman, "Can Asia Bounce Back?", April 1998

It was once said that the business of America is business, and what is good for business must be good for America. By this logic, good corporate governance leads to good public welfare and bad corporate governance must be bad for the economy as a whole. Ergo, Asian crisis. 

 

Corporate Governance

Corporate governance is broadly defined as a set of relationships among key participants in the corporation, principally 1) the management 2) the board of directors and 3) the owners and other "stakeholders" by which the broad objectives of the company are determined, the means of reaching those objectives are agreed and performance is evaluated*. The stakeholders include shareholders, creditors, employees, consumers, regulators, management and the public who could be affected by activities of the corporation.

* Draft paper on "Towards the Development of International Standards and Guidelines for Corporate Governance", OCED 1998.

A good corporate governance structure, like a balance sheet, is a stock concept that delineates the rights and responsibilities of each group of the stakeholders of the corporation. More often than not, we are more concerned about the flow concept of *process* of corporate governance, where internal and external incentive structures hopefully would lead to checks and balances, better decision-making, monitoring and performance. The internal incentive structures have to do with the delineation of responsibilities, internal checks and balances between the stakeholders and how these impact on goals of profitability, efficiency and performance. The external incentive structures relate to compliance with the laws, regulations and increasingly global standards of behaviour and best practices. 

Given the rapidly changing world, even the standards are constantly evolving, so that good corporate governance is itself a moving target. As this conference has amply confirmed, we can neither fully agree on a "*one size fits all*" what constitute best practice, nor how do we arrive at best practice.

However, we do know that in the absence of good benchmarks of governance practices, corporate performance could be subject to abuses and misuse that is sub-optimal to society as a whole. So what promotes good corporate governance? It is obviously multi-dimensional in nature, touching on legal, historical, regulatory, behavioural and even cultural aspects of corporate practices.

Nevertheless, the fact that we have rapidly moved from the Cadbury recommendations of 1992 to draft OECD Guidelines for Corporate Governance today means that we have come a long way towards understanding the nature of the concept and the issues. Increasingly, we are moving towards a global consensus of what constitutes principles of good governance and a code of best practice. Several key elements can be identified:-

- *Objectives* - such as maximizing shareholder value through meeting the demands of stakeholders;
- *Transparency* - the provision of information to stakeholders for them to assess performance against their expectations;
- *Benchmarks* - measuring performance against laws, regulations, standards or codes, which are national and increasingly global in nature;
- *Accountability* - the checks and balances within the corporate structure that ensures that performance is measured against the benchmarks and objectives.
The common glue amongst all the above is information, the raw material of markets. Transparency requires timely and readily accessibility of relevant information. But information has to be measured, classified and analyzed, requiring standards or benchmarks of measurement, valuation and judgement. Moreover, there is a process of accountability by which performance is judged against these benchmarks, in which the agents are rewarded or sanctioned by the stakeholders.

Modern markets could not have achieved their present level of liquidity, depth and volatility without the rise of information technology. Hence we need to look at the role of transparency, information technology and governance.

 

Transparency

My understanding of the importance of transparency has benefited greatly from my privileged vantage point as co-chair of the G-22 Working Group on Transparency and Accountability*, with Mervyn King, Deputy Governor of the Bank of England. The Working Group was helpful in that it drew from the experience not only of the developed (G-10) markets, but also expertise from the emerging markets which had first hand experience of the recent financial turmoil. The practical recommendations we made applied not only to the national authorities, international financial institutions, but also the private sector.

* Report of the Working Group on Transparency and Accountability, October 1998, available on IMF, BIS and World Bank websites.

Transparency refers to a process by which information about existing conditions, decisions, and actions is made accessible, visible, and understandable. It helps to promote accountability, by obliging decision-makers to make their decisions and the reasoning behind them known, and furthermore, to be answerable for such actions and decisions. Transparency is thus a check and balance and monitoring device, to ensure that stakeholders understand and can influence a company's performance and operations in their and the public's interest. 

Markets need information to function well. Shareholders need information to judge the quality of their investment. In the absence of disclosure and openness, bounded rationality leads to behavioural patterns such as "follow the leader", herding, acting on partial information and assumptions, etc., which can lead to contagion and market dislocations. Transparency is thus an important element to the efficient functioning of markets.

When actions and decisions are made visible and understandable, and the objectives and benchmarks by which the institution is judged made clear, poor decisions will not go unnoticed or unquestioned. Efforts to enhance transparency and accountability can thus help insulate firms and countries from systemic risks and contagion and avert a build up of financial imbalances and risk concentrations.

The Working Group identified two serious gaps in transparency that were apparent in the Asian crisis. The first was *gaps in disclosure*, especially private sector information on indebtedness and short-term capital flows; and relevant and timely data on official external reserves position, including short-term debt. The second was the *gap in incentives to use available information*. Even though information was readily available to predict potential fragility in selected Asian economies, many banks, funds and corporations chose to ignore such warning signals.

There was perhaps one additional gap that, in my opinion, was not well explored: the gap in *capacity* to understand information. Through technology and innovation, modern financial markets have become far too complex for many corporate leaders, policy makers and regulators to fully comprehend their implications for the bottom line and for public policy. This led them to be too sanguine about risks, on the assumption that if you don't understand it, don't tinker with what appears to be a good thing. As we are all aware, Barings was an a prime negative example of what went wrong when senior management did not understand where the apparent profits were coming from. LTCM was another example where even the most sophisticated regulators did not appreciate the systemic risks of non-monitoring of large positions by hedge funds.

Thus, there is an important public good in investor education, just as there is a high return on investing in staff education. The greater the understanding of the individual rights and responsibilities of the investor, the employee, the consumer, the corporation and the authorities, the more efficient are the markets. Information thus helps to delineate property rights and responsibilities, on which markets thrive. The education of the market participants, from investors to intermediaries, is helpful to the working of the market.

 

Benchmarks or Standards

The delineation of property rights can only be achieved with better defined standards of measurement and valuation and clear set of regulations and laws. Transparency is only useful if there is adequate and timely disclosure of objectives and standards by which performance can be judged. Information is classified by accounting standards. In the absence of generally accepted accounting standards, uniformly enforced and applied, it is impossible to compare and contrast corporate performance globally. A useful dictum from the Asian crisis is:-

Bad Accounting   = Bad Statistics

                           = Bad decisions

                           = Bad risk management

                           = Bad policies and monitoring

                           = Financial fragility and crisis

 

In classic IT terminology, garbage in, garbage out.

There is of course, very useful work being undertaken in by the International Accounting Standards Committee (IASC) and the International Organisation of Securities Commissions (IOSCO) to develop consensus on objectives and elements of private sector disclosure. The Working Group emphasized the important elements of timeliness, completeness, consistency, risk management and audit and control processes.

However, we are no longer simply debating about the need for global standards in accounting or asset valuation, but also the degree of formality of conduct or performance. These range from basic principles, guidelines, best practices, codes of conduct, regulations and finally laws.

In short, we have global markets, but local or national laws and regulations that may not be in harmony with the needs of globalization. How these local standards, codes of conduct, regulations and laws begin to converge towards global standards will become the most difficult political and culture transition of the 21st century. Indeed, the whole debate of Asian values versus Universal values fall into this category of controversy.

As an international financial centre, open to the whole world and a major player in global markets, Hong Kong must meet global standards of disclosure and performance. This is the sine qua non of globalization.

 

Information Technology

What has IT got to do with transparency and corporate governance? As every CEO who has dealt with the pains of computerization found out, you cannot make the transition from a paper-based organizational structure towards a modern IT-integrated knowledge-based institution without a clear understanding of the corporate objectives, standards of performance, internal controls and internal and external data-base management. Those who computerized old paper-based systems without thinking this through are now condemned to the dust heaps of corporate failure. The Wal-marts and Deutsche Terminal Bourse of this world learned how to integrate technology into traditional lines of business to create new standards of performance.

Thanks to ever-advancing technology, information is now readily available in almost every shape and form to enable transparency and accountability to function to their full effectiveness.

IT makes possible the processing and transformation of huge quantities of complex data and information at a relative low cost. It is forcing standardisation of objectives, measurement, processes, and benchmarks so that units of operations can be monitored and controlled more quickly and efficiently. Simple hash-totals, for example, ensure accounting consistency. Standardization of accounting reporting formats enable performance to be monitored functionally, and across time and geography. Technology helps better risk management through the use of sophisticated risk models.

At the same time, information technology has altered profoundly the relationship between shareholders, the company, intermediaries, and the public. Companies are forced to be more open in order to reach to the global market. Data availability through the Internet ensures that shareholders have better and faster information to hold their corporate directors and managers more accountable for their action. Through technology, no board of directors can have an excuse that they do not have access to the right information.

Cyberspace, however, transcends geographical boundaries and hence geographical governance. It is something that national laws and regulations are still grappling with.

Like all tools, IT is only a means towards an end - the objective of good corporate governance. While IT can help us get towards Nirvana faster, it has its downside, as the Y2K problem and dangers of system failure only demonstrates.

 

Accountability

There is increasing recognition that the speed of emergence of new financial products, procedures and practices makes the existing systems of corporate governance, both internal and external, less effective.

The OECD Code sees great advantage in the role of independent non-executive directors or supervisory boards, with strengthened powers of internal and external audit. There is greater consensus on disclosure-based accountability, where corporations, like governments, have to operate in full bright sunshine.

Regulatory authorities have also become aware that traditional means of enforcement form of regulation in markets, using criminal codes, are extremely costly and ineffective*. No amount of sophisticated regulatory system can prevent financial failure, nor the abuses and scams brought about by inadequate corporate governance. The classical rationale of regulation to protect "widows and orphans" generates its own costs in the form of moral hazard.

* See thrust of United Kingdom Financial Services and Markets Bill, July 1998.

One way to minimise moral hazard of rigid regulation and the inability of regulators to prevent all abuses is the "caveat emptor" approach, to increase transparency such that market participants will have the incentives and the information to take care of their own interests. To do so, the rights and responsibilities of each stakeholder, including the regulators, must be made more clear and more transparent, so that the regulator must be subject to the same standards of accountability as those that are being regulated. 

But in the end, there are limits to transparency, which is a necessary, but not sufficient condition for efficient markets. Information is by definition costly to compile, and proprietary and private information cannot be disclosed for competitive and privacy reasons. Regulators cannot disclose supervisory information without compromising client confidentiality. There is therefore a necessary trade-off between the benefits and costs of transparency.

In the final analysis, it is the human factor or the ethics of good governance that guides and ensures good corporate behaviour. High transparency reinforced by information technology, accountability reinforced by transparency in the best and most sophisticated of markets have not prevented corporate crime or financial failure.

Corporate governance, like all governance, is all about a matter of trust between two parties. It boils down in the end to the fiduciary duties of the directors or management, and even the auditors and regulators. SEC Chairman Arthur Levitt puts it brilliantly in his recent speech, and I end with this quote:

"In this information-driven age, the director's responsibilities have become more complex. But the director's role rests on an old-fashioned value: integrity. Integrity of character. Integrity of information. Integrity of auditing and compliance systems. And above all, integrity of mission."

Thank you.
