In response to recent media enquiries and reports, the Securities and Futures Commission today makes the following statement:

On 21 July, SFC Executive Director of Enforcement, Mr Mark Dickens, said at a media gathering that the SFC and the Stock Exchange of Hong Kong were conscious of the need to deal with the increased risk of misconduct by directors of listed companies because of the financial crisis. It was thought that because of the economic downturn, some controlling shareholders and directors of listed companies might come under considerable financial pressure and therefore might be particularly tempted to engage in insider dealing in anticipation of bad news to avoid losses. Mr Dickens said there was also an increased likelihood of breaches of fiduciary duties for example by entering into transactions designed to move assets or losses and liabilities from one company to another or from a listed company to controlling shareholders' hands. He added that the SFC and the Stock Exchange would discuss how to identify and closely monitor companies that were most at risk. In response to a question from the media, Mr Dickens said that based on the difference in market situation between 1993-94 and 1997-98, it was estimated this could apply to 40 or 50 smaller listed companies with their controlling shareholders under financial pressure. Mr Dickens stressed that it was only an estimate. He also added that it did not mean there was necessarily going to be misconduct among them.

On a number of public occasions last week, SFC Chairman Mr Anthony Neoh was repeatedly asked by the media to identify the so-called 40 or 50 companies. Mr Neoh reiterated that the number was only an estimate based on experience of past difficult years. He said the SFC together with the Stock Exchange exercised constant surveillance over the markets to look at unusual share price movements, which might indicate an unfair or disorderly market. At all times, the SFC had a number of companies under specific surveillance and enquiries. Mr Neoh said while financial conditions might create temptation for insider activities or corporate misgovernance, it did not mean a company under surveillance was in fact in financial difficulties or that there was actual insider trading.

If a company is experiencing such financial difficulties that its results are very likely to be affected, a listed company should consider disclosing the necessary information and if it is uncertain whether it should do so, it should consult the Stock Exchange. Under the rules of disclosure of the Stock Exchange, a company must keep the Stock Exchange and the investing public informed as soon as reasonably practicable of any information relating to the listed company and its subsidiaries (including information on any major new developments in the sphere of activities of the listed company and its subsidiaries which is not public knowledge) which:

(a) is necessary to enable them and the public to appraise the position of the group;

(b) is necessary to avoid the establishment of a false market in its securities; and

(c) might be reasonably expected materially to affect market activity in the price of its securities.

The Stock Exchange on 9 July issued a reminder to all listed companies that in the light of potential deterioration of earnings, companies should make timely and appropriate disclosures. Any company found not to have abided by the requirements will be dealt with according to the rules. At the same time, should there be fraud or other market malpractice, they will be investigated and dealt with according to law by relevant agencies, including the SFC and the Police.
