The Chairman of the Securities and Futures Commission, Mr Anthony Neoh, today gave his personal views on effective financial regulation in a speech at the Sixth Conference on Pacific Basin Business, Economics and Finance, which is being held in Hong Kong.

Mr Neoh commended the Stock Exchange of Hong Kong for having established itself as a leading market for capital raising, and for employing state-of-the-art trading and settlement technology.

He added that, however, the Stock Exchange had not been as effective as it should be in acting as a self-regulatory organisation in supervising its members, despite the hard work of successive Chairmen, Chief Executives, their staff, and many of the Council members.

Mr Neoh said the SFC and the Government would work closely with the Stock Exchange and the brokerage industry to formulate measures to restore investors' confidence in the market.

Mr Neoh also noted that the International Organisation of Securities Commissions (IOSCO) had on Wednesday published for public consultation a set of "Objectives and Principles in Securities Regulation".

Common to all the core principles is the concept of an operationally independent regulator who is accountable for the exercise of its functions and powers but is not subject to political influence.

The IOSCO principles consider that effective implementation of self-regulation is a necessary and important ally to the securities regulator.

In particular, the principles require the regulator to ensure that self-regulatory organisations (SROs) enforce their own rules and impose appropriate sanctions for non-compliance.

"Whereas the regulator and the SRO act as the enforcer of standards by appropriate supervision and enforcement activity, there is also general recognition that financial regulation must rely in the end on good management by the firms themselves," Mr Neoh said.

Address

by

Anthony Neoh, SC

Chairman, Securities and Futures Commission

to

The Sixth Conference on Pacific Basin Business,

Economics and Finance

on

28 May 1998

"The Core Principles of Financial Regulation"


As I had written this address on the plane coming back from Paris and I had to come from the airport immediately to this speaking occasion, I have not had time to discuss the views I am about to give with my colleagues. These views are therefore mine and mine alone, and they do not necessarily represent the views of the Commission.

As we near the end of the Millennium, let us pause for a moment to take stock of a phrase that recently rang loud on the international stage. That phrase was that we have seen in the East Asian Financial crisis, the first crisis of the 21st Century. That became a resonating statement because it captures two important warnings:

(1) That the effects of this crisis will certainly be felt for some time into the 21st Century; and

(2) That the lessons learnt in the crisis may well in time give way to human frailties and the crisis is likely to repeat itself in some other time, in some other form and, perhaps in some other place.

In a millennium of human progress, we have seen the wonders of technology brought about by advances in science and forms of social and economic organisation which have brought about the concentrations of wealth needed to catalyze and create the progress we have seen. Yet in the light of all this progress, human nature does not seem to have changed. The corruption that grows from un-checked power remains with us. Human greed, and credulity accompanied by greed remains with us. The modern term for this: is the quintessential 'fast buck'. Quick money in abundance and at no risk, seems to be the 'holy grail' of many. You may think that that is too good to be true, but where social and economic organisation is such that the few is favoured against the many, and power is concentrated in the hands of the few with no accountability to the many, this 'holy grail' is sometimes attainable, and has in fact been attained in history. It is only human nature to seek comparative advantage, and the best comparative advantage is monopolistic economic and better still, political power. Paradoxically, it is this tendency that pushes the world toward vast concentrations of capital to enable research to be undertaken and technology to be created. But it is also the pull of labour and consumer acceptance and participation that has enabled the fruits of technology to be widely spread.

Thus if we look at the so-called 'Asian miracle', we will find that the early stages of development have been marked by vast concentration of capital in the hands of a few with the masses enjoying only a slow build up of the prosperity, but as savings and skills level increase, part of that wealth began to percolate to the masses. Today in Asia, we have renowned universities, world class hospitals, a population that is increasingly becoming skilled and educated, it enjoys savings rather highest in the world, demographically, each of the Asian nations holds young and dynamic populations. That, of course, has been the very stuff that the Asian miracle has been made of. Yet, if we pause to consider the crisis that has so violently assailed us in the past year, we will have to admit that there is much that has not changed.

The World Bank, in its 1998 Global Development Finance Report, states that the financial crisis in East Asia stemmed from a build-up of vulnerabilities during the 1990s and a self-perpetuating loss of confidence.

The World Bank Report goes on to state that the key problems (which gave rise to the financial crisis in East Asia) was in the financial sector, where poorly managed financial liberalisation, distorted incentives, inadequate disclosure, lax regulatory standards, and inadequate supervision encouraged excessive risk taking, particularly through maturing and currency mismatches. Financial sector weaknesses permitted a misallocation of investment and a build-up of non-performing loans. Large capital inflows amplified the problems of the financial sector. But, as the Report also points out, the increase in vulnerabilities does not fully account for the spread and subsequent depth of the crisis in East Asia. The severity of the currency and stock market declines, coupled with the limited forewarning of the crisis from market participants, is strong evidence that a self-fulfilling loss of confidence played an important role.

I would venture to suggest that embedded in the phenomenon described by these measured words is a deep malaise which afflicts, in varying degrees, not only the economies of East Asia but perhaps, a much wider geographical area in the Region. This malaise is what Paul Krugman has called 'crony capitalism'. We see this in various forms in Asia. In its most malignant form, political power is nakedly exploited for personal economic gains, such as that we have seen in Indonesia where the populace has finally said, 'enough is enough'. In its milder form, political power is used to sap the vitality of law enforcement or regulation in the financial sector and as a result, weaknesses are perpetuated and economies grow weak.

Even in Hong Kong, where there is a high degree of accountability of its public servants, not enough has been done to hold the specially privileged or those with vested interests to public account. I am, for example, deeply disappointed and saddened as a member of the financial community by a report in yesterday's SCMP of certain statements made by the financial community's recently elected representative and a former Vice-Chairman of the Stock Exchange. The former was reported to be against brokers being asked to compensate investors and the latter was reported to have publicly said that he would lead public protests if brokers are asked to contribute for compensation to investors who have invested with failed brokers. The recent failures of the brokers have been either the result of mis-management or theft of their peers. One would have thought that the members of any trade who have profited, and profited richly, from years of patronage of their clients, would have at least have rallied together and found ways to make good the losses caused by the irresponsible or the fraudulent from among their number, and thereby earn the confidence that has been reposed upon them in the past. Even the owners of cake shops, realizing that confidence in their trade might be at stake, rallied together to honour the customer obligations of one of their fallen comrades.

Though I am disappointed at the statements of these two leaders of our brokerage community, I am however not surprised. That is the natural result of years of privileged existence, when the monopoly that they enjoyed has enabled them to exact rents from the community which are growing untenable as each day passes. For every $100,000 that is transacted in the Stock Exchange, $7 is collected for the Stock Exchange from investors. Yesterday, the Stock Exchange collected over $1 million. Undoubtedly, the Stock Exchange provides one of the most important markets to the Region and in turn, it has benefited the community of Hong Kong. Whilst the Stock Exchange has carved out a name for itself as an important market for raising capital globally and excels in its trading and settlement technology, it has never been as effective as it should be in acting as a Self-Regulatory Organisation in supervising its members. Despite the hard work of successive past Chairmen, Chief Executives and their staff, as well as many of their Council members (of which I was one), it remains a Club for brokers, dedicated, it appears, to serve that interests of brokers perhaps a bit more than investors. This is not an occasion to pillory brokers (the community will hold those of them who do not deserve their confidence to account soon enough, and all right thinking people would acknowledge that the present Chairman, many Council Members, as well as the Executive Staff of the Stock Exchange, works hard enough as it is to do what they can to earn the public's confidence in the Exchange), but I cite this as an example, that even, an international market such as Hong Kong, has much room for improvement. I am certain that the Chairman and Chief Executive of the Exchange must already be hard at work formulating proposals to instill confidence in the Exchange's members and I, as well as the Government, will be working closely with them to assist them in their important task. If this discussion about brokers seems a digression, let me bring you back to my main thesis. ***It is that a sound financial structure must be built on just foundations, where the interests of the few must not dictate the interests of the many. This philosophy is at the core of all financial regulation****.*

But having principle alone, which might act as a call for action, is insufficient to achieve any lasting result. Therefore, there has been a determined effort among financial regulators worldwide to devise a guide to action. The Basle Committee for Banking Supervision published its "Core Principles for Effective Banking Supervision" in September 1997. In the same month, the International Association of Insurance Supervisors adopted the "Insurance Supervisory Principles". Yesterday in Paris, IOSCO published for public consultation, the "Objectives and Principles of Securities Regulation" which is the culmination of a year's solid work by many members of the Organisation.

There is general recognition by all these world financial regulatory groupings that financial regulation must exist within an adequate public infrastructure where there exists adequate laws (including corporate, bankruptcy, contract, consumer protection, anti-competition and private property laws) and adequate judicial and dispute resolution processes. There must be an effective accounting and inadequate auditing system. It is also recognized that the reduction of systemic risk is the role of all financial supervisors. But at the same time, reduction of systemic risks should not mean that institutions should not be allowed to fail. Saving a mis-managed institution will give distorted signals to the market, encouraging excessive risk taking. It is therefore recognized that since it is possible for the occasional failure to occur (and the task of supervision is to provide the proper incentives for firms to manage themselves properly and thereby prevent failure as much as possible), there may well be a need for a safety net, such as insurance schemes, to protect the smaller investors.

Common to all these core principles is the concept of an operationally independent regulator who is accountable for the exercise of his functions and powers but is not subject to political influence. This therefore requires the responsibilities of the regulator to be clearly and objectively stated, that he shall have on the one hand, adequate powers, proper resources and on the other, he should adopt clear and consistent regulatory processes, and his staff should observe the highest professional and ethical standards.

Particular to the IOSCO principles is the recognition that the securities regulator cannot bear the sole burden of regulation. For regulation to be effective, the industry must pull its weight. Principles have therefore been proposed for effective implementation of self-regulation as a necessary, and important ally to the securities regulator. The guiding principle has been stated thus : "Once the SRO is operating, the regulator should assure itself that the exercise of this power is in the public interest, and results in fair and consistent enforcement of applicable securities laws, regulations and appropriate SRO rules". In particular, the Principles require the SRO to, among these things, enforce its own rules and impose appropriate sanctions for non-compliance and to avoid rules which may create uncompetitive situations.

Whereas the regulator and the SRO act as the enforcer of standards, by appropriate supervision and enforcement activity, there is also general recognition that financial regulation must rely in the end on good management by the firms themselves. It is clearly in the interest of financial intermediaries to manage themselves properly so as to instill confidence in themselves and in their industry as a whole. It is therefore implicit in any sound system of financial supervision, that the first line of defence always lies with the firms who should accordingly be guided by Rules of Conduct and entry and continuing professional as well as financial requirements which are fully transparent, so that their clients should be able to make informed investment decisions and be aware of their rights, and be able to hold their chosen intermediary to account by way of appropriate channels of complaint to the SRO and the regulator.

In a world which is increasingly becoming integrated, it is now essential that financial regulators across borders and across sectors should co-operate. There is therefore a strong call for such co-operation in all core principles. In particular, the IOSCO principles pick up all the themes set out in the 10 key principles to information exchange devised by the G-7 Finance Ministers in Birmingham two weeks ago.

As markets increasingly globalise, there is not only the need for co-operation and information exchange, but also a need for much greater attention to be placed on improving 'transparency', not only in the processes of regulation but also in the processes of the market. The IOSCO principles propose important benchmarks for primary and secondary markets as a framework for the continuing technical work of the organisation and pursuant to which, IOSCO, also published in Paris yesterday, the IOSCO Disclosure Standards for Cross-Border Securities Offerings.

The Basle Committee's Core Principles of Banking Supervision and the IOSCO Objectives and Principles of Securities Regulation in particular are not only substantial tomes, the former containing 25 and the latter 30 basic principles which are elaborated upon in commentaries, but they also now represent the best extant benchmarks against which financial regulation may be judged in each economy. Economies which are seen to comply will benefit in terms of higher levels of investment and lower costs of funding. They also represent an immense challenge to the regulatory community to practise a system of high standards. Only time will tell, whether the world's financial systems will improve as a result of these standards, but this concluding story, at least to me, offers hope.

I shall soon retire to Peking University to rest my weary mind, at least for sometime, before I return to my practice at the Bar. At Peking University, there is on permanent and prominent display a statue of Miguel Cervantes, who as you know, is the author of Don Quixote. I was surprised by this as I knew that Peking University excelled in English rather than Spanish studies, so I asked an old, and wise friend, why Cervantes and not perhaps Shakespeare, was given such pride of place. What my friend said will always be an inspiration to me. He said that Cervantes was commemorated because of the undaunting spirit found in the character of Don Quixote. Like Don Quixote, countless Chinese intellectuals over the centuries and particularly those of this century, have set out to do battle for justice knowing full well that they had little chance of success but nonetheless, they sallied forth in the knowledge that, if they fell, their example will spur the next on, to carry on the fight for another day, and, when the fortune of the nation changes, the battle will be won. That is the spirit of courage and self-sacrifice that I will commend to my colleagues in financial regulation.
