Address to the Institute of Directors

by

Anthony Neoh, SC,

Chairman, Securities and Futures Commission,

on 29 September 1998

 

"Where do we go from here"


 

On the eve of my leaving my post as the third Chairman of the Securities and Futures Commission, I am particularly pleased that I am given the opportunity to address an organisation which I believe is of great importance to our community. A joint stock company constitutes one of the most important vehicles in the modern world by which capital is formed and directors play the crucial role of guiding the destinies of companies. Collectively therefore, directors of companies determine the prosperity of our community, which is why I believe you are a particularly apposite group of persons with whom I would like to share a few parting thoughts.

As company directors, you must be acutely conscious that your shareholders would only continue to have confidence in retaining their investment if they feel that there is an appropriate environment in which the business of the company can prosper and in which they can feel that their rights are fully protected. Indeed, Mark Mobius, a well known international fund manager, has frequently said that fund managers would more likely than not invest in markets which are Fair, Efficient, Liquid and Transparent. He uses the acronym FELT. Recent press reports have it that he and other fund managers are heavy in cash and are pondering their investment options in Asia and in other markets.

From a global perspective, investment funds now exceed US $21 Trillion. The lion's share of these monies have remained in the United States, followed by European G-7 countries and then, Japan. Markets outside the G-7 countries, have received a minor fraction of these funds but that has been sufficient to fuel an emerging market boom in the last decade. It is true that in the light of the financial crisis that has hit Asia since last year and the continuing spread of the contagion to almost all emerging markets, particularly those in Russia and Latin America, investment funds from the G-7 countries have been significantly repatriated, but it must be borne in mind that in the long run, Asia and other developing regions have demographics in their favour.

All G-7 countries have populations which are ageing much faster than the populations of Asia and other developing regions. The Governments of G-7 countries have long realised this and know full well that their countries would not be able to sustain state provided pensions to their future populations. Thus, they have been pursuing taxation policies which have favoured savings and investment with the intention of reducing the pension burden on the state. These policies have now given rise to a fund management industry which has burgeoned and continues to grow. The markets of the United States and Europe have flourished in the wake of this growth of investment funds. However, industry pundits have begun to question whether listed companies in these markets will be able to sustain the earnings required by their market valuations. I would not venture to provide answers to these queries but I would say this: whatever are the answers to these queries, since investment money always seeks the highest return commensurate with risk, it will not be long when institutional investors, who are kept constantly abreast of developments in other markets by a highly competitive global financial press and financial research industry, will spot the market which begins to offer potentially better returns on risks which they can reasonably assess.

By virtue of the mere fact that their market valuations have been so drastically reduced, all markets afflicted by the current financial crisis offer, potentially good returns, but the important element in an investment decision is risk assessment. That is why the four elements in the acronym FELT are so important because they are the benchmarks by which all investors, particularly institutional investors, will base their decision to re-enter the markets of Asia and other regions.

I have dwelled on the global perspective because we must constantly remind ourselves that capital knows no ideology, patriotism or national boundaries. Every market which relies heavily on external capital must compete for its capital in the international arena. Hong Kong is clearly one such market. Even in the depressed state of our market, our stock market capitalisation exceeds our Annual GDP. Therefore, our listed companies have relied, and relied heavily, on international institutional investors. Successive stock exchange surveys in the past have shown that the turnover in Hong Kong's stock market has, save for the first three quarters of 1997, been dominated by institutional investors, over half of whom are overseas institutional investors and even those institutional investors who are local have a large international clientele. It has been commonplace for many of listed companies to make private placements of their shares in the international markets. Since 1993, initial public offers in the primary equity markets may be made globally and there have been some major global offerings, one notable example being the raising of US $4.2 Billion on October 23, 1997 by China Telecom.

That our economy has been able to grow at the rate it has in the past is the result of a ready supply of international funds joining our local savings pool and intermediated through the stock market and the banking system. This ready supply of capital has not only financed our businesses but has brought in its wake, a large financial and professional services industry plugged into the international markets. The present credit crunch in our economy has been the result of the retrenchment of international funds, leaving us to our own savings pool. In time, our own savings pool will be intermediated into investment but if we do not have international funds, our economic recovery will be longer in coming. If recovery is too long in coming, we may lose a part, and possibly a vital part of our finance and professional services industry, bringing us into even deeper recession as financial and professional services represent some 25% of our Annual GDP. Thus, so that we do not run the risk of going into further recession, we must convince international investors that the conditions which have made it attractive for international funds to be invested in Hong Kong, will continue to apply. Here, Hong Kong continues to have a great deal to offer.

Despite the fact that our economy is contracting, it must be remembered that we are contracting from a high base and arguably from an asset bubble in both the property and equity markets of 1997. At the same time, no other economy in Asia has a still strongly growing economy in its hinterland. The economy in China is still growing and while inflation is still decreasing, interest rates have come down and may still come further down, reducing the real interest rate and allowing credit to fuel the economy. Investment, particularly public investment, is continuing, adding to employment and growth. Foreign direct investment for the first 8 months of this year came to US $27.4 Billion (a decrease year on year of 1.5%) but contracted FDI increased to US $31.7 Billion, representing an increase of 6% year on year. 35% of these direct investment flows have come from the US, Britain, Germany, Canada and Australia. The Chinese Mainland economy has been relatively untouched by the Asian financial crisis and a vast domestic market awaits not only the entrepreneurs of the country including those of the Hong Kong SAR but the entrepreneurs of the world. This offers vast business opportunities to Hong Kong companies. At the same time, this offers vast opportunities for the financial markets of Hong Kong.

As the public sector of the mainland economy is re-structured and as the private sector grows (and it is growing strongly), the thirst for capital will increase. In the past five years, Hong Kong has raised some US $30 Billion for 41 State owned enterprises and about 55 so called Red Chip companies and another US $5 Billion in about 100 B share companies. These are small numbers compared to the great number of enterprises in the mainland. This is enough to make any investment banker rub his hands in glee but let me immediately provide him with a reality check. Is there any immediate business? The answer is "none!" or at best, "very little!".

The Asian financial crisis has made international investors highly risk averse and the liquidity of our equity markets has been substantially attenuated by a transfer of a very large block of the "public float" into government ownership. I have no idea how big a portion of the public float has been removed from the markets because the government's holdings have not yet been disclosed. However, since our market efficiencies continue to be among the best in the world and we are continuing to improve our trading and settlement systems, liquidity will return sooner or later, so long as investors are able to assess risk reasonably, for then, they will be able to price in the growing business opportunities in the mainland in their evaluation of our markets. We should therefore devote ourselves to ensuring that they can adequately assess this risk. Again, we come back to the acronym FELT (Fairness, Efficiency, Liquidity and Transparency).

As financial markets are markets of information, a good system of disclosure and a consistently applied set of market regulations will enable investors to price in underlying business risks efficiently. The World Bank in their World Capital Markets Report published earlier this year found that investors were unable to differentiate between good and bad investments because the markets in some East Asian countries did not have sufficiently good systems of disclosure and they therefore left these markets wholesale, notwithstanding that some of these markets may still have well run and viable companies. To enable us to be the first market to recover in the financial crisis, we must ensure that our markets undoubtedly possess the two important attributes of a good system of disclosure (namely, transparency) and consistency of application of market regulations (namely, fairness). In this regard, I welcome the Financial Secretary's decision to disclose the government's holdings as soon as the present state of emergency passes. At that point we should have a much better idea of the public float in each listed stock.

I also welcome the Financial Secretary's decision to hold the government owned shares in a corporate entity, at which point, the market would expect the managers of this corporate entity to comply, and be seen to comply, with all relevant market regulations both in relation to disclosure and dealing. As soon as it became clear that the government was to become the owner of a large block of the public float, I had advised the Financial Secretary of the need to ensure that the market perceives public ownership of these shares as not conferring any more status then private ownership. I am pleased that the Financial Secretary had positively responded to this advice and I have every confidence that as soon as it is practicable to do so, the two decisions I has just adverted to will be followed through.

I am however somewhat alarmed at the proposal to seek legislative changes to the present regulatory system to confer powers on the Chief Executive to give directions in an emergency relating to the monetary system to exchanges and clearing houses without, it seems, the intervention of the market regulator. It may be that in the time available for formulating the bare proposal, it has not been possible to think through the full ramifications of the proposal nor was it possible, it seems, to formulate the proposal in a form which allows for more certainty to the markets. Whilst no one doubts the good intentions of those making this proposal, I would caution that discretionary directive powers conferred on the political head of the government, even in an emergency, would tend to detract from the need to provide certainty to the markets. Thus it has been the universal trend of market regulation to vest clear and objectively stated powers of market regulation on an operationally independent but publicly accountable market regulator. This trend has, of course, been recognised since 1989 in the establishment of the Securities and Futures Commission as an operationally independent but publicly accountable public body. The need for clear and objectively stated regulatory powers and for operational independence and public accountability of the market regulator is also recognised in the Objectives and Principles of Securities Regulation adopted earlier this month by the entire 150 member organisations of the International Organisation of Securities Commissions ("IOSCO"). These Principles also state that the regulator should adopt clear and consistent regulatory processes.

The Principles represent the most comprehensive set of international standards hitherto available in securities regulation. All of these standards go to ensure that market participants are able to operate in an environment of certainty which thereby provides one of the essential conditions for growth. I am heartened that the Government is continuing with a constructive dialogue with the Commission and I am therefore confident that my successor and the Government will wisely find a solution which provides certainty to the markets and yet gives the Government sufficient comfort that emergencies can be adequately dealt with. I would add that this optimistic view stems in part from the existence of a power conferred by Section 11 of the Securities and Futures Commission Ordinance on the Chief Executive to give such directions to the Commission in writing as regards the performance of any of its functions as he considers appropriate and it is also stated in the Ordinance that the Commission shall in performing its functions comply with the Chief Executive's directions.

As we address the above issues, we must not forget that the markets inexorably march on, and if we do not continue to refine and invigorate our regulatory structure, we lose out in the competition for international capital. With the assistance of the IMF, Thailand and Korea are fast reforming their financial systems. Singapore has recently substantially revamped its regulatory system to consolidate its position as a leading international financial centre of Asia. London has just published a well-crafted, but more importantly, far-sighted Financial Services and Market Bill which will bring their financial regulatory structure into convergence with the foreseeable trends of the future. Hong Kong must not be left behind.

There is much that needs to be done to build a sound future. If we are to go into the 21st century with sound foundations, we must now work hard to finalise a new legislative platform for our regulatory system. London, realising this, has published theirs. Ours was published in 1996 and had undergone two public consultations. In the past year, we have, together with the Government, worked hard to refine the substance and structure of the Composite Securities and Futures Bill. I am informed that a legislative slot has been allocated for that in 1999.

But as we craft the legislative platform of the future, it has already become plain that when it is finally enacted, there will be provisions which do not fully capture the needs of a fast developing market. One significant area lies in remedies. Apart from insider dealing, our regulatory laws rely largely on disciplinary and criminal sanctions. Disciplinary sanctions only assist in regulating those who need to be authorised practitioners. Criminal sanctions have been uniformly low, having been fashioned in the 1970's, and do not therefore represent much of a deterrent. This is being addressed in the Composite Bill but in any event, it is often not easy to prove a case in our legal system to a standard of 'beyond reasonable doubt', and people who have already been involved in improper conduct, often go free.

The United States have long realised that criminal sanctions cannot represent the full panoply of remedies and have since 1934 empowered the SEC to institute civil actions against persons involved in improper market conduct. We have in our insider trading laws, remedies akin to civil remedies, disgorgement of profits together with penalties potentially up to three times the profits made or loss avoided. Civil remedies are useful because they take away the very gains which constitute the motivation for misconduct. With civil sanctions, because the individual's personal liberty is not threatened as in criminal sanctions, the law allows a lower, but still high, standard of proof. Financial misconduct is, we all know, easily hidden in layers upon layers of machinations which are difficult to detect, let alone link up into a coherent criminal prosecution case. Placing the criminal standard of 'beyond reasonable doubt' on the community on all but the most egregious cases would not be fair on the community. Thus, the Commission has started to research the application of civil remedies to all cases of market misconduct. As to this, we have the US experience, our own in insider trading cases and the UK published proposals. It is a matter of regret to me that these legislative reforms cannot be completed within my tenure. However, at least, I had the comfort of knowing that my colleagues and my successor will vigorously pursue these reforms in the future. That in the end is what is important. I had been proud to continue with the agenda of the Commission when I took over. There is vast continuing agenda of work in the Commission. I have no doubt that my successor will bring to this agenda, new thoughts, new dimensions, and renewed creativity.

In conclusion, I commend to you the following thought found in the opening paragraph of a memorial by the First Prime Minister of the Tang Dynasty to the First Tang Emperor: "If a tree were to grow tall, then its roots must be well firmed, if a river were to reach far, then its source must be well irrigated."
