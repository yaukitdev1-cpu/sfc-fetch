Listed renminbi securities, when available, will involve a clearing and settlement process that is different from their HK dollar counterparts. In the latest issue of[ *InvestEd Intelligence*](http://www.invested.hk/invested/en/html/section/focus/mar11/sounding.html), the Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) reminds investors to take steps to get prepared before investing in this upcoming class of products.

Investors, who plan to subscribe to renminbi securities in an initial public offering, should first open a renminbi bank account. Furthermore, they should verify if their intermediaries are on a list to be published on the website of the Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing Ltd giving names of intermediaries that have completed successfully a series of tests and confirmed readiness for trading listed renminbi securities. Meanwhile, they can find out more about renminbi securities from a list of [frequently asked questions](http://www.invested.hk/invested/en/html/section/products/RMB/trading.html) posted on the SFC’s InvestEd website.

“Just as we asked intermediaries to go through a series of tests to confirm their readiness for this new class of products, we now remind investors to make sure they are ready first,” said Mr Martin Wheatley, the SFC’s Chief Executive Officer. “Investors should understand the trading requirements specific to listed renminbi securities, and ask their intermediaries to clarify anything unsure.”

Penning this issue’s “Sounding Board” column, Mr Mark Steward, the SFC’s Executive Director, Enforcement, urges investors to play a part to avoid mis-selling, starting with helping their intermediaries understand their individual circumstances and asking questions until they fully understand how a product works and its associated risks.

The newsletter also discusses equity-linked investment (ELI), an example of a structured investment product that is more complex than meets the eye, and reveals to investors the downside risks and maximum loss they may face in the worst-case scenario. Another article explains why a company’s share price may fall after it announces capital re-organisation, often accompanied by a rights issue, and what implications this has for shareholders.
