The International Committee on Listing of New Enterprises (the Committee) today published the executive summary of its report dated January 2001. The Committee, comprising international market experts and prominent individuals from the Hong Kong market, was formed in April 2000 to conduct an independent study into the key factors that make a listing venue attractive to issuers, analysts and investors. Given the interest at that time in the qualifications for listing growth enterprises, the study had particular regard to Hong Kong's Growth Enterprise Market (GEM) and similar markets in the US and Europe. An important objective of the study was to ensure the continuing competitiveness of Hong Kong as an international financial centre. 

The Committee conducted the study by:

- examining Hong Kong-headquartered companies that have listed overseas in recent years;
- comparing the regulatory regimes, governance and legal frameworks that pertain to GEM and other major markets, using Nasdaq's National Market, Frankfurt's Neuer Markt and London's AIM and techMARK boards as a benchmark; and
- interviewing key decision-makers in 30 organisations characterised as either 'issuer-based' or 'investor-based'.
On the basis of its study, the Committee made a number of observations in relation to GEM and offered the following recommendations: 

1. in view of the tendency for local (and Mainland) issuers to list on 'home soil', moves geared to maintaining and enhancing the quality of GEM would not necessarily deter issuers;
2. retaining a unified GEM that caters both to companies with an established track-record and issuers with recognised growth potential was preferable to segmenting GEM's platform into two sections: one for companies with 'active business pursuits' or track-record and another for 'start-ups';
3. the level at which listing rules is set, particularly in relation to provisions like 'lock-up' and 'track record' is a relevant factor but this must be evaluated against the wider statutory and regulatory framework. In relation to track record, the Stock Exchange of Hong Kong (SEHK) should review whether GEM should remain with the existing criteria (of 'active business pursuits'), which provides for some flexibility on the issue, or build in one or more specific profit, revenue or other qualitative requirements. As a further variation, a hybrid of both approaches could be considered. In any event, track record criteria should be measured against the material business(es) listed;
4. SEHK should consider changes to GEM's market microstructure, given the success of dealer-based trading systems elsewhere. In particular, by inviting market-makers to quote firm prices, and allowing investors to trade-off transaction costs for immediacy, some liquidity gains may emerge for even the smallest cap stocks; and
5. SEHK should re-appraise the size of the 'free-float' for GEM companies and the concentration of shares amongst placees. Evidence elsewhere indicates that liquidity improves with the size of the free-float. While there are difficulties in broadening the base of placees, so that the largest placees take up a smaller proportion of the issue, measures to do precisely this would probably promote liquidity (even in the absence of a market-making system). Further study should be given to this issue.


The Committee stressed that the central message, applicable to all markets, is the importance of ensuring quality in market structure and administration. 

The Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) and the SEHK welcome the recommendations of the Committee and have taken account of them in the review of the GEM listing rules which has been ongoing since May last year. The results of that review are being announced separately today.
