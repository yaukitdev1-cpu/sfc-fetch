On 24 September, Mr Paul Bailey, Executive Director of Enforcement was telephoned by a Robert Tankai purporting to represent a company called Kearns Investments Inc. ("Kearns"). Mr Tankai said Kearns was based in Japan and asked if Mr Bailey was interested in investment in US stocks and outlined briefly the services Kearns could offer. Following a brief conversation, Mr Tankai said he would have one of his senior consultants to call him. 

Two days later, on 26 September a person identifying himself as Mr Maxwell Kane telephoned Mr Bailey. He explained that Kearns offered investment services - although they liked clients to invest 95% of their funds in blue chips shares and fixed income products, they also offered investments in venture capital companies. Mr Kane then identified a company with shares quoted on the NASDAQ over-the-counter market. He said he had some inside information from someone in the company that being in the business of fire resistant building materials, the company was about to obtain contracts to rebuild the area around the World Trade Centre in New York. He explained that when the market in New York dropped 100 points on 25 September, they had managed to acquire a few million shares in the company at US$0.5 each. The market then rebounded and the share price closed at US$0.75.

Mr Kane said he could offer Mr Bailey 100,000 shares of the company at US$0.5 each, on the basis that this was an incentive for Mr Bailey to open an account with Kearns. He could not however explain why Kearns was willing to give up the US$0.25 profit per share they had already made. When asked about the registration status of Kearns in Japan for conducting a business of dealing in securities, Mr Kane was unable to answer. He cut the call short saying he would call Mr Bailey back.

A check of the trading of the company on NASDAQ indicated that during the period 20 to 25 September, the daily turnover never exceeded 200,000 shares and at no time did the share price hit US$0.5.

Comment on this occurrence, Mr Bailey said, "This event is factual but raises a number of issues. Firstly, it is illegal for a person to attempt to induce people in Hong Kong to purchase, or deal in any way, in securities without being registered with the Commission. Kearns nor Mr Tankai nor Mr Kane are registered in any capacity with the Commission. Secondly, the representations made in this case appear factually incorrect, especially as to the source of shares. Also, one would question why Kearns would be willing to sell to an unknown person, and a person who was not a client of Kearns, shares at such a deep discount to the market price apparently enabling the buyer to make an instant profit. However, looking at the trading pattern of the NASDAQ company, and given the large number of shares apparently available from Kearns, those seeking to sell may find this could have a considerable downward impact on the share price."

Mr Bailey added, "It is important that investors, or any member of the public, receiving such calls make proper inquiry. For example, they should -

- check whether the company soliciting business is properly licensed in the jurisdiction they operate and the country which they target their business;
- check the background of the company whose shares are being offered and whether or not their sales pitch is true. Nowadays many financial websites give access to quotes of shares traded on overseas markets and information/news concerning these companies;
- beware of anyone who says they have inside information. Insider dealing is a criminal offence or civil wrongdoing in all major financial markets;
- beware of any investment under offer that appears to give you an immediate promise of large profit; and finally
- do not remit money or agree to anything until you have made proper inquiry."
