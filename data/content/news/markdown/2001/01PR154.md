The Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) wishes to warn registered persons of the high risk of fraud if they allow client accounts to be misused and investors of their potential liabilities if they allow third parties to operate their accounts. According to reports received by the SFC and the Police, a number of registered persons and investors are suspected to have been the victims of this type of fraud and have suffered losses as a result. The Commission and the Police are currently investigating these reports. 

The Commission wants the industry and the public to be aware of how accounts are typically misused so that they can help prevent similar abuse from occurring in the future. Registered persons are reminded that they are obliged to comply with all regulatory requirements in order to promote the best interests of clients and the integrity of the market.

A typical case begins with a third party introducing one or more new customers to a dealer's representative in a brokerage. However, the dealer's representative only meets the introducer, not the customers and thus does not know whether the customers have really agreed to accounts being opened on their behalf. In some cases, we have established that the introducer has used stolen identification for himself and the "customers".

The dealer's representative proceeds to open cash accounts in the name of the individuals but operates the accounts largely or exclusively on instructions from the introducer without any written authorization. Trading commences in a single stock or a small number of stocks, all of which are illiquid. While all appears well to begin with, sooner or later the customers suffer losses, sometimes very significant. The brokerage then pursues the customers for repayment. The customers complain they have not authorized the trading which led to the losses in question. Meanwhile, the introducer disappears. The stockbroker then faces the prospect of pursuing a disputed claim.

In other cases the SFC has found that customers have actually agreed to lend their identities or accounts to third parties without appreciating that their accounts may be used for market manipulation activities by a number of parties including the introducer, his associates and even account executives.

The scenarios that we have highlighted are common in the market today. Regrettably, it appears that some brokerages and their employees have chosen to ignore the fundamental principles of the Code of Conduct for Persons Registered with the Securities and Futures Commission in their efforts to attract new business.

The Commission's Executive Director of Enforcement Alan Linning said: "In failing to establish the true and full identity of the client, registered persons have failed to observe the Code of Conduct. As an employer, registered persons are held responsible for the conduct of their employees. The lack of internal controls and an effective compliance programme will lead the Commission to question the fitness and properness of the registered persons. In acute cases, registered persons will not only suffer the financial consequences of their negligence but also risk having their licenses suspended or revoked by the SFC particularly where market manipulation is involved."

"There is no good reason why a new customer's account should be operated by someone else, still less reason for someone to be opening an account on behalf of a new customer. Brokerages are reminded that it is their responsibility to know their clients: taking reasonable steps to establish the true and full identity of the client, each client's financial position and investment experience and objectives. The brokers cannot fulfil this responsibility if they haven't even met the new customer. Alarm bells should ring when attempts are being made to keep a broker away from the customer," said Alan Linning.

"To investors, our message is: don't let anyone use your account or identity in any circumstances; monitor the transactions conducted through your accounts; and ensure that you receive and review contract notes and transaction statements promptly. If investors knowingly allow third parties to operate their accounts, they may be liable for any losses suffered. Worst of all, they may attract criminal liability if their accounts have been used for market manipulation," Alan Linning said.
