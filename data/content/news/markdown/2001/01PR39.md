The Securities and Futures Commission announces that the [Consultation Paper on a Review of the Codes on Takeovers and Mergers and Share Repurchases](http://www.sfc.hk/sfc/doc/EN/speeches/public/consult/takeovers%20consultation.pdf) is released to public today. This consultation paper proposes various amendments to the Codes and invites public comments on these proposals. The consultation will last until 31 May 2001.

The current Codes were introduced in 1992. Other than the introduction of Rule 2.10 on privatisations by scheme of arrangement in 1993 and "housekeeping" reviews in 1997 and 1999, there has been no overall review of the Codes since 1992.

The policy of the Commission is to keep the Codes under regular review with the aim of ensuring that the Codes are kept in pace with the market developments and international practice. There have been significant changes in our market since 1992. In particular, typical transactions here have moved away from "controlling shareholder privatisations" to a broader selection. Our market has also become more international, in terms of both transactions and market participants. Additionally, a number of weaknesses in the Codes have emerged over years of application and practice, which ought to be remedied. With these in mind, the Commission proposes extensive amendments to the Codes, including a complete reorganisation of the Share Repurchase Code.

One item of the proposals put forward for consultation concerns the trigger (which is the level of control of a company that triggers a mandatory offer for an acquiring shareholder) and creeper (within which a controlling shareholder may increase shareholding without triggering a mandatory offer). The Commission proposes lowering the trigger from the existing 35% to 30%, and reducing the creeper from the existing 5% in a 12 month period to 2%.

The trigger was introduced into the Codes at 35% in 1981, together with the creeper, initially at 3% (in any 12 months) but increased to 5% a few months later. Since then, there have been views in the market that regard 30% as a more realistic level at which effective control of a company passes. UK has adopted the 30% trigger since 1974. Similarly, the 30% trigger is included in the Securities Law of 1998 in the Mainland. Singapore's trigger is 25% and Malaysia's is 33%. The proposed change in the trigger will bring Hong Kong more in line with the Mainland and other international markets.

The creeper was originally adopted in Hong Kong to allow major shareholders to make market for shares, thereby increasing the share liquidity. However experience has not demonstrated that major shareholders actually make market nor that the creeper has been widely utilised. Using other markets, such as Singapore, that are similarly characterised by controlled and family companies as guide, the Commission believes that the 2% creeper in 12 months is reasonable.

To avoid prejudicing shareholders unfairly with the new trigger, the Commission recommends that shareholders who hold between 30% and 34.9% of a company on the implementation date should only abide by the existing 35% trigger so long as they themselves stay within this band. These shareholders will be asked to register their interests with the Commission. The proposed grandfathering provision would be in place indefinitely subject to continuous review. The rationale for this proposal is that no person should be advantaged or disadvantaged as a result of a rule change. However, concerned with the possible market confusion in maintaining two levels of trigger, the Commission also seeks public comments on an alternative that the grandfathering provision should apply only for a fixed period of time, say 10 years, after which all shareholders would be subject to the same trigger and creeper.

To phase in the new creeper, the Commission proposes to apply the new 2% creeper to the period extending from the relevant acquisition back to no further than the implementation date, while the original 5% creeper to the one year period running back from the relevant acquisition. This transition will last for one year from implementation.

Other proposed amendments of the Codes include rationalising the voting requirements for schemes of arrangement to acquire or privatise a company and for delisting a company, clarifying the level of acceptance for compulsory acquisition, and strengthening the requirements for asset valuation.

To enhance minority shareholder protection in privatisations by way of a scheme of arrangement, Rule 2.10 of the Takeovers Code, as introduced in 1993 and revised in 1998, requires the approval of 90% in value of independent shareholders present and voting, or the disapproval of no more than 2.5% of the total shares. Because the rule, particularly the 2.5% test, has been seen to be unduly restrictive, the Commission proposes to relax the rule so that the rule requires approval of 75% in value of the disinterested shareholders present and voting and disapproval of no more than 10% in value of all disinterested shareholders. The Commission also proposes to extend the voting requirements to cover not only privatisation of a company by its controlling shareholder, but also acquisition of a company by any person.

To address the market concern over delisting being used as a threat to pressure minority shareholders into accepting privatisation by way of general offer, the Commission proposes to impose the same voting requirements on voluntary delisting of any company as privatisation by scheme of arrangement.

The Commission also considers adding Rule 2.11 to make it clear that 90% of the disinterested shares (i.e., shares other than those held by the offeror and its concert parties) have to be acquired before compulsory acquisition may be exercised under any companies law.

In view of the numerous business valuations of technology or internet oriented companies having been prepared and published in recent takeovers and mergers, the Commission proposes to expand extensively Rule 11 of the Takeovers Code. The proposed Rule 11 will require valuations based on business or financial valuation methods or models to be made or reported on by financial advisers. The rule also clarifies that valuations prepared on a discounted cash flow basis are regarded as profit forecasts and, if published, will need to be reported on by accountants and financial advisers in accordance with Rule 10 of the Takeovers Code.
