Attached for your reference is a speech given today by Mr Andrew Sheng, Chairman of the Securities and Futures Commission, at CS International Business Committee Lunch.

 


***"Technology, Change and Reform***

Implications for Hong Kong's Securities and Futures Markets"

(Speech presented by Mr Andrew Sheng, Chairman,

Securities and Futures Commission

at CS International Business Committee Lunch

on 22 April 1999)

 


The Hon. Mrs Anson Chan,
Mr George Legros,
Ladies and Gentlemen,

 

I am very honoured to be invited here today to say a few words on the securities market reforms. Much has happened since the reforms were announced on Budget Day by the Financial Secretary and a lot of work is going on. What I propose to do is to give you an overview of the impact of technology on financial markets, its challenges and implications for Hong Kong's securities markets, and therefore the need for reform.

 

Technology

The American nuclear scientist, J Robert Oppenheimer, said at the advent of the age of nuclear energy 50 years ago:

"This world of ours is a new world, in which the unit of knowledge, the nature of human communities, the order of society, the order of ideas, the very notions of society and culture has changed, and will not return to what they have been in the past. What is new is new, not because it has never been there before, but because it has changed in quality."

As we embark on the dawn of the new Millenium, these words reflect our puzzlement as we witness the age of Technology. Technology has profoundly altered the way we do business in the securities industry. Consider the following:

If cars decline in price at the same rate as price of PCs computing power, the price of a car would be US$5;Internet financial transactions cost US 1 cent per transaction versus traditional paper-based transaction costs of US$1;Before 1995, Internet trading did not exist. Today, 10 mn investors can choose from over 100 online brokers in the US alone. Online trading now accounts for 25% of all trades on the NYSE and Nasdaq.Through eSchwab, discount broker Charles Schwab opened 1.4m new accounts during 1998. Four years ago, its market cap was US$3.5 bn, but today, it is US$41.5 bn, equal to the market cap of the Thai market, and larger than Merrill Lynch and Boeing.Currently there are 8 traditional stock exchanges registered with the SEC, but there are more than 20 private electronic trading systems where institutional traders can trade without touching a traditional exchange. 

Change

I quote all these examples because technology is changing the whole ball game. Technology brings change, confusion, competition and consolidation. But it also offers creativity and opportunity. It cuts out the traditional middle-man, but brings value to the consumer and investor. This is why firms that are willing to apply Web technology to their business are being rewarded in terms of larger market capitalization. This is why we have seen the merger of banks with insurance companies, asset management companies and securities houses. This is why we are witnessing open outcry trading floors being replaced by screen-based trading. Through technology, the large fund managers which control nearly US$8 trillion worth of portfolio assets can offer the whole range of products and services to the global investor to suit their individual risk appetite.

At the same time, the innovative small business can access global markets through Internet trading, as Amazon.com has shown.

What we need to understand now is that financial markets are essentially networks. Financial markets are networks across which property rights [securities] are traded and transferred. The network has externality. The bigger and wider the network, the more value because it allows more participants access to more information, more counterparties and more property rights. The more information you have, the more you are able to manage your risk, the more you can make a good decision of whether to buy, sell, or hold.

The network model implies that markets function very well with high transparency, low transaction cost, which leads to high liquidity and low volatility. Transparency, robustness in infrastructure, clear and fair regulations, low transaction costs are the major reasons why the leading financial centres such as London, New York and Hong Kong maintain their leadership. But with technology, only the paranoic survive.

Competition and consolidation of exchanges

Technology drives competition through diversity, quality of service, timeliness and easy access. Technology drives down costs, but at the same time, investing in technology is expensive. Consequently, what Hong Kong has witnessed since the Asian crisis is not only increasing price competition, but also direct product competition. This is most prominent in the attempt of SIMEX to launch the MSCI Hong Kong Index (HiMSCI+) in November last year.

As listings become global, as in the case of HSBC when it lists in New York in addition to London and Hong Kong, trading will be on a 24 hour basis. The extension of NYSE trading hours to 19 hours would mean that Hong Kong shares can also be traded in New York time, at NYSE trading costs.

Hence, exchanges are battling over price, efficiency and liquidity in an effort to become the key price discovery hub in their own time zones of the global trading network. The ability of the Deutsche TerminalBourse to capture the German Bund futures contract from the London International Financial Futures Exchange (LIFFE) in less than six months, which forced the merger talks between London and Frankfurt is a vivid example of the twin forces of competition and consolidation. This has now prompted Paris, Madrid and Milan to discuss the creation of a Pan-European stock exchange.

Alliances of exchanges are not confined to Europe. The Chicago Metal Exchange (CME), MATIF and SIMEX announced that they would form an alliance spanning three time zones, offering round-the-clock trading in some of the most actively traded financial derivatives on a single electronic platform by June 1999. This would link some 2000 trading screens in Europe, the US and Asia, adopting essentially the French trading technology and the CME's clearing system.

Such rapid consolidation of exchanges coupled with high costs of technology exerts enormous pressure on smaller exchanges whose incomes depend on a narrow product range. This has resulted in the demutualization of exchanges in Stockholm, Amsterdam and Australia, with plans for demutualization and public listings being planned in Athens, London, Singapore and Toronto. Demutualization allows the exchanges to respond more rapidly to market forces and listing enables them to tap new capital from the markets to finance the heavy investment in technology. At the same time, being part of the larger network of exchanges in different time zones enable them to cut costs and access greater trading opportunities.

As the Financial Secretary, Donald Tsang, said in March, there are at least eight clear benefits from the reform initiative:-

- **First, the existing member has the additional choice of exchanging his membership rights for new shares in the holding company, without sacrificing his trading rights.** The listing of the ASX suggests that the shares of a premier exchange could be financially very attractive.
- **Second**, Hong Kong would gain a **new flagship financial services blue chip** that would widen the range of investments in Hong Kong.
- **Third, consolidation** of the Exchanges and Clearing houses within a single entity would bring **economies of scale** in terms of operational efficiency, savings in infrastructure investment, facilitation of risk management, and financial strength to meet external competition.
- **Fourth, demutualisation and public listing** would ensure that the Exchanges and Clearing Houses would act not only in their members’ interests, but also be **responsive to market forces**. Demutualisation would result in professional management and organizational streamlining. Public listing should encourage the market institutions to raise funds and subject themselves to **transparency, accountability and market discipline**.
- **Fifth,** under the **simplified management structure**, there will be greater attention to new businesses, such as the Growth Enterprise Market and the sale of information on listed companies, or the provision of back-office services to small brokers.
- **Sixth**, as a public listed company, the new entity can **raise new funds to upgrade and concentrate on its technological platform** to deliver the most open, efficient, robust and integrated financial infrastructure required to compete globally. It would facilitate electronic straight through processing and provide sound risk management facilities across the spectrum of new products.
- **Seventh,** the current **regulatory functions** which are presently shared between the Exchanges and the Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) would be **rationalized to remove overlaps**, **improve efficiency** and reduce regulatory costs. This would allow the SFC, the Exchanges and the Clearing Houses to concentrate on their **areas of expertise** and allow the market operators to concentrate on commercial needs, unhindered by potentially conflicting regulatory responsibilities.
- **Eighth, the energies currently engaged in resolving internal conflicts would be devoted towards meeting external competition and strengthening efficiencies.** The new entity would be large enough to negotiate alliances and market strategies with regional and global exchanges from a position of strength.
Since March, we have all been working very hard to put these reform measures into more concrete shape. There has been considerable public consultation and valuable comments and constructive suggestions from many quarters. All this is very healthy. All these suggestions and comments will help make the work of the financial advisers and all those involved in the planning that much easier.

We have received considerable cooperation from all stake-holders concerned. Whilst all of us are impatient with the need for information, valuations, projections and detailed issues to be provided as fast as possible, I think it is only realistic to give the technicians time to work out the facts and figures on a consistent, comparable and professional basis. We recognize that there are many private interests at stake, but we are sure that all would want a fair deal that is for the greater good of Hong Kong. We at the SFC are working closely with all parties to ensure that we will meet the deadlines on time.

Maintaining the status quo is not an option for Hong Kong. The proposals for reform has in my view has stirred up a healthy re-assessment of the deep seated issues and challenges facing the securities and futures market in Hong Kong. It has induced us to seriously evaluate our competitive strengthness and weaknesses. We may agree. We may agree to disagree. But from this cauldron of creative and open discussion, I am confident a new consensus for reform will emerge.


 - E N D
