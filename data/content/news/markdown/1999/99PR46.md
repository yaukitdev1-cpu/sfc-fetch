**"Accountability and Transparency in the Age of Global Markets"**

 

(Speech presented by Mr Andrew Sheng, Chairman,
Securities and Futures Commission
at The American Chamber of Commerce -
 Financial Services Committee Luncheon Meeting
on 14 May 1999)


I am very honoured to have the opportunity to speak to you today. 

I am asked to speak to you on transparency and disclosure in the age of global markets. Transparency, disclosure and accountability are very big words today. They are certainly the flavour of the times. What I will do in the next 20 minutes is to take you through the transparency, disclosure and transparency issues that confront our market and the Securities and Futures Commission today, and explain why they are crucial to the continued development of Hong Kong as a international financial centre. I will also describe how we at the Commission will do our part to ensure that not only are these standards steadfastly observed in our markets but also within the Commission itself as the regulator of these markets.

Globalization

I shall start by painting for you the global landscape in which the Hong Kong markets operate today. Unless one sees this full picture, it would be difficult to understand why transparency, accountability and timely disclosure are so crucial to the continued health and vibrancy of our markets, and to Hong Kong's bid to continue as an international financial center. It will also illustrate the difficulties that we face in a very confused and changing world, brought about by increasing globalization and technological innovation. One of the reasons why we are preparing a new draft of the Securities and Futures Bill is for us to thoroughly re-examine ourselves within the Commission what is our new role in the fast changing market. I believe these challenges are not just those facing the Commission and the market but, if I may say so, all institutions within Hong Kong and Hong Kong itself.

In October 1997 the Asian financial crisis swept the shores of Hong Kong. It was a crisis of globalization. Since the early 1990s, a huge amount of money was poured into Hong Kong and the rest of Asia, creating a big bubble. Then, suddenly, we find ourselves in the midst of a painful recession.

So, what does globalization really mean? It clearly means a quilt-like world where different markets have been linked into a single network or market that trades around-the-clock foreign and domestic securities worldwide. HSBC Holdings announced that it would be applying to the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) for listing, bringing about global trading of its securities in Hong Kong, London and New York. In short the market for HSBC shares could now trade 24 hours. We also see in the newspapers that the NYSE is going to trade until midnight, which means that they will be trading in the morning hours of Hong Kong, when the market starts trading.

Globalization is the end product of deregulation, financial innovation, and most of all, technological advancement. The financial reality of globalization, however, is that it has brought about considerable change, intense confusion, tremendous complexity and fierce competition, not just within the region but on a global level as well.

Technology

I mentioned technology as one of the catalysts of rapid change, and globalization. Take, for instance, the Internet. Internet banking transaction costs US 1 cent per transaction, against traditional paper-based banking costs of US$1. So, at one-hundredth of the cost, Internet banking can take away all the traditional businesses. The same will be true for Internet stock trading or e-Commerce and anything that could be done through the Net. It will change the whole ball game. This explains why we see banks merging with insurance companies, asset management firms and securities houses.

Technology has increased in leaps and bounds the speed and consequently volume of transactions. Take for instance the computer, the most tangible symbol of the metaphysical might of technology. Estimates today tell us that within 24 hours of computer downtime, a financial institution would lose half its business. 72 hours of computer downturn, it would be out of business.

The more far reaching implication, however, of technological innovation is that cyberspace is transcending geography. You can conduct e-Commerce from the middle of the Gobi Desert as long as you have a website. Your cost could be the lowest in the world but you could nevertheless trade with anybody anywhere so long as he has an internet address.

The speed, innovation and sophistication of world markets have made it possible, and indeed necessary, for them to seamlessly merge into one giant round-the-clock market where an investor can trade shares of a Frankfurt-listed company on NASDAQ from his computer screen in Sydney for settlement in London.

Globalization also brings a blurring of traditional franchises. Whereas traditionally investors have had to trade on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange, now they can trade via their computer screens the same shares but in a different market. If that market provides better service and lower costs, the franchise gets chipped away. Thus, existing within the monolithic single world market and competing with it are a fragmented world of ATSs (alternative trading systems) and ECNs (electronic communications networks), many catering to different trading communities and offering different add-on services, often at very low costs.

I read in the May 12 issue of the NY Times that Island ECN, a computerized stock-trading service that opened for business less than three years ago dealing with brokerage firms and offering a system that automatically matches up orders from buyers and sellers without going through a dealer or trading floor, applied in late April to the SEC to become America's newest stock exchange! It has been 26 years since the SEC last approved an exchange.

ATSs and ECNs such as Island are luring trades away from the NYSE and NASDAQ by offering quick service and miniscule fees. Older markets fight back by offering longer trading hours, demutualizing, investing in technology and establishing alliances aimed at making themselves more competitive.

Networks

Financial markets are essentially networks. Through their underlying technological infrastructure, these networks disseminate information and transfer property rights among investors and across markets and national boundaries. Looked at in this manner, even a physical stock exchange is also a network.

Networks have externality. The bigger and wider the network, the more value it provides because it brings into the system more participants, more information, more property rights. Thus, for Amazon.com, the wider the coverage, the greater the number of customers, the greater the value of the network. Anyone from anywhere in the world who wants to find a book but does not know where would go to Amazon.com. Therein lies the value and reach of the network.

Networks that can efficiently disseminate information also help investors to manage risks. Markets are really information driven. The more information an investor has, the more he is able to manage his risk, assess his returns, protect his property rights and make that educated decision of whether to buy, sell, or hold.

The more efficient a network, the greater the requirement for accurate and timely information and transparency. Markets function best with high transparency and low transaction cost, which leads to high liquidity and low volatility.

If markets are networks, and world markets merge into one another, then we must place the Hong Kong securities market in the context of these networks. The Hong Kong economy is like a local area network. You can call it an Intranet. People communicate effectively on the Intranet. Immediately beyond but linked to this Intranet is an Extranet, representing Hong Kong's advantageous business linkage to Greater China. By feeding into the Intranet, the Extranet brings to us all the advantages, as well as all the risks and complexities, of the Mainland market. Investors wishing to plug into the Extranet for Greater China logically see the Hong Kong hub as the window for entry.

What is the true value of Hong Kong? Why do people come to Hong Kong? Hong Kong's value lies in its open and pluralistic society and its international access. You have freedom of information, a fair system of justice, good corporate laws, International Accounting Standards, a low tax base, and specialist knowledge on China. Hong Kong is the centre of efficient information flows and open technology. It also interfaces between Greater China and the rest of the world. Hong Kong can be used as a packaging or entry center for China investments and a risk management center for those investments.

Hong Kong is a fund raising centre for China as well. Being a hub in the global economy, the Internet, Hong Kong offers China easy access to the rest of the world. Our role is to perform this hub function within the Extranet of Greater China and with the growing Internet worldwide network.

Transparency, Accountability and Disclosure in the Market

So what have all these got to do with transparency, accountability and disclosure? Why do we need them?

To maintain integrity and to function fairly and efficiently, the market needs high quality information, timely disclosures and efficient access to such information. Investors need this information to make investment decisions and to trade. When relevant information is not properly disclosed in a timely fashion, when insiders abuse their positions and misuse information, or when misleading information is given, this will destroy market fairness and integrity and the level playing field. Investors will shy away from investing in the market.

Technology and information are always changing. Often the market is a better judge than the regulator with respect to new innovations and products. A market that enjoys ready access to accurate, high quality information has the tools to properly assess new products and innovations and to digest their risks and returns and their full legal implications.

Transparency requires not only the disclosure of information, but also a whole host of other supporting benchmark standards designed to facilitate the dissemination, use and protection of the information and the investors' property rights. These include good accounting standards (to accurately assess value and risks), clear law and regulations fairly and consistently administered (to provide certainty and protection of property rights) and good corporate governance (to ensure proper conduct and safeguard investors' interests).

History has shown that price and liquidity of shares of companies with poor corporate governance, opaque operations and unreliable accounts tend to be the ones that suffer most whenever there is any rumour or economic uncertainty. That is a function of lack of transparency.

Hong Kong prides itself for being open and transparent. But the Asian Financial Crisis has revealed gaps in the information that we need to deal with crisis.

Gaps in information

The Asian financial crisis has demonstrated that we have gaps in disclosure, gaps in incentives to use information, gaps in the law and regulatory net and gaps in capacity to use information. Take last August for example. The double play in Hong Kong was possible because we have bifurcations in the regulatory net. HKMA looks at the currency market. SFC looks at the securities market. As far as the hedge funds or the investors are concerned, the two are just one market. So, if we do not have the information to understand how hedge funds operate, how can you deal with them? Hence the establishment of the Cross-Market Surveillance Committee.

The case of CA Pacific is another example. There was a regulatory gap in that while brokers are regulated by the Commission and financial institutions by HKMA, there were no regulatory constraints imposed on the activities of margin finance companies, except for some loose rules on money lenders. Also, there was a legal lacunae as the law was not clear on exact property rights with respect to shares held in a general pool.

The fallacy of composition gave us an illusory comfort of safety. While everyone had pieces of the information, no one had the whole picture. Let's take a simple illustration. Every development in town would be billed as the best property with the best location, would be in heavy demand and appreciate in value due to scarcity of supply. What was omitted from the calculation was that if every developer built these properties, there would be an oversupply relative to demand. This is the fallacy of composition. Transparency will ensure that all information micro and macro is provided so that everybody has a fair chance to assess the prospects and risks. I began to appreciate these issues when I co-chaired the G22 Working Party on Transparency and Accountability.

But we must remember transparency is not an end in itself. Transparency entails a price. Transparency and disclosure have high cost because information has high cost. Why should a person in the private sector who has proprietary information disclose it? There would always be a balancing and trade-off between the high cost of collecting information, analyzing information, and using information and the need to disclose the information for the public good. This tension would never be resolved completely and will always be there. And these are the issues that every market and every community face.

Plugging the Gaps

The securities ordinances that the Commission administers, some of which are some 25 years old, have become outdated amid rapid market and technological developments. We are now in the course of revising the Composite Securities and Futures Bill to update these laws, to close the gaps and to build the regulatory infrastructure that can cope with the demands of the 21st century. The work is still going on but I can point to the general direction of the reforms.

The new regulatory regime will seek to enhance standards of transparency, accountability and disclosure. Where necessary, contractual rules and voluntary codes will be given statutory backing.

In the Bill, the Commission will be asking for a rationalization of its powers. This will include the right to impose civil fines on intermediaries for misconduct. At the moment, the Commission's disciplinary options are very limited. It may reprimand (often seen as ineffective a sanction), or suspend or revoke a license (in substance a nuclear solution with drastic consequences, especially on innocent third parties). Civil fines would be an intermediate sanction and if applied appropriately could be quite effective. The SEC and CFTC both have the power to impose civil fines. The UK is proposing that the FSA also be given the power to fine intermediaries as one of its disciplinary tools.

The Bill will also give the flexibility to enable new products and activities to be properly categorized and hence dealt with under the appropriate regulatory net. With the seismic shifts that informational technology and telecommunications have brought and will continue to bring, it is necessary that the Commission be given some flexibility in its rule making powers to make rules to respond promptly to new needs. The Commission will also be issuing new codes and guidelines to help it discharge its functions.

These give you a flavour of the changes that are currently considered. I shall not dwell on them any further. Instead, I would like to discuss a more fundamental issue, the role of the Commission itself in the current climate of change.

The Role of the Commission

In the law reform, we need to think about our role. What are the objectives? What are we trying to achieve? What kind of market do we ultimately want? What are we trying to regulate? How do we want to regulate?

Regulatory objectives

First, the regulatory objectives. Basically, with this broad picture of confusion, change and competition that I have just painted, what should we be doing? The regulatory objectives established by IOSCO in the securities area are helpful. They are:





- protection of investors
- ensuring that markets are fair, efficient and transparent
- reduction of systemic risks.
We believe it is important to set down in the Bill our regulatory objectives, as these objectives constitute the vision and purpose of our regulatory regime. These objectives also go to the issues of accountability and transparency. We are seeking to rationalize our powers to effectively regulate the market. These powers must be exercised in furtherance of our regulatory objectives. In other words, we must set for ourselves clear benchmarks against which the exercise of our powers can be openly and objectively measured for fairness, proportionality and consistency. Moreover, whether the Commission has effectively performed its functions should be able to be assessed by reference to these benchmarks.

Our regulatory objectives are:





- maintaining and promoting confidence in the securities industry
- maintaining fairness, efficiency, competitiveness and transparency of our markets
- promoting the understanding of the investing public of benefits and risks
- seeking the appropriate degree of protection for investors
- minimizing financial crime and misconduct
- reducing systemic risks.
Fairness and Due Process

As a regulator within changing markets, we have to deal with new and complex issues, but it is natural that the legislature instinctively recoils from giving the regulator more powers. Gone are the comfy days when government bills sailed through calm LegCo waters. We are a pluralistic society where broad consensus has to be built before a new law could be passed. We must earn the powers that we seek by opening ourselves to a high degree of transparency and accountability. We have to be fair and equitable in our approach and fully observe the rules of due process. In fact we already practise a very high standard of due process.

Currently, the checks and balances that are in place against possible abuse of the Commission's powers are:





- There are an equal number of executive and non-executive directors on the Commission board. The NEDs oversee the work of the Commission and act as the first line of independent supervision of the Commission's executive functions.
- Enforcement and licensing decisions of the Commission are fully appealable on the merits to an independent appeals tribunal.
- Aggrieved parties can seek judicial review of a Commission decision.
- The Commission is also subject to the scrutiny of the Ombudsman and the ICAC.
- Commission's accounts are audited independently and tabled before the LegCo. The Director of Audit has the right of access to the Commission's records.
- Annual estimates and annual reports of the Commission are required to be laid before LegCo. Officers of the Commission regularly appear before the LegCo Panel on Financial Affairs to answer questions.
- The Commission maintains a website on which it posts accounts of its performance.
- No new law is laid before LegCo for deliberation and enactment without prior public consultation.
- The Commission has established internal procedures that ensure that persons regulated by it are afforded due process, treated fairly and given the right to present his case before a final decision is made.
Process Review for More Accountability

The work of the Commission is market sensitive and prominent, as we are essentially dealing with high profile financial crime. There is an inherent fear that we are protecting the big at the expense of the small man. Given that some of the decisions that we make could affect a person's reputation and means of livelihood, it has been suggested to us that in addition to the accountability and due process protection that is currently available, we should consider adopting further measures, such as instituting a separate review committee to review the process of the Commission, to ensure that we adhere to clear, transparent procedures and there are sufficient safeguards against bias, unfairness and arbitrariness.

We are of course receptive to these suggestions. In our drive to reform Hong Kong's securities law, it is important that we ourselves are, and are seen to be, willing to reform ourselves. The questions for us are how we could balance the public interest of more transparent accountability against the risk of creating too many levels of review that would adversely impact on the Commission's effectiveness and independence. We believe the answer lies in identifying those of the Commission's decisions that are vulnerable to challenge, and establishing an efficient system of review of the processes that lead to these decisions.

Before I conclude, perhaps I could revisit the point I made earlier about the role of the regulator. It is to keep the market clean, transparent and fair. We are the investors' friends. We try to protect their interests. However, the best regulation cannot prevent market crime and market failure, and the government cannot insure all investors against loss. So, prevention is better than the cure, and therefore, ultimately, the market works best when each investor takes responsibility for his own decisions. We need to understand that each part; each participant in the securities market must have its own clear role and responsibilities. In our work, we need to have due regard to the continuous innovation in the market; and that over-regulation can shift the markets overseas to Singapore, Shanghai, or Sydney. Over-regulation can prevent beneficial innovation. We need to engender fair competition among everybody. It is imperative therefore that we have transparency in regulation, within the limits of our secrecy and confidentiality and we must also be very efficient in use of our scarce resources .

In conclusion, globalization is a complex, competitive and ever-changing issue; and Hong Kong needs a flexible, sensible and user-friendly regulatory framework. What we hope to achieve through the new Composite Bill is to maintain Hong Kong's competitiveness in an increasingly competitive global financial world, and we can only do this with the co-operation and support of the financial community.

Thank you very much.
