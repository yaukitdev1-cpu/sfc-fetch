The following are remarks made by the Chairman of the Securities and Futures Commission, Mr Andrew Sheng, at a year-end meeting with the media today:

1999 turned out to be a year of recovery, following a year of correction in 1998. During the year, the Hang Seng Index gained 6,913 points, or 69%. Turnover has recovered by 253% from 1998 to 1999 and reached $18 billion on 3rd January 2000, making us one of the best performing markets in Asia in 1999. Our market capitalisation has reached US$608 billion, the 10th largest in the world. Average daily turnover of HSI futures was 16,011 contracts, 38% lower than 1998.

1999 was an extremely challenging and busy year. We can call it a year of major reforms: the main focus of the work of the Commission was the three-pronged financial market reforms announced by the Financial Secretary in March in his Budget Speech. I am happy to note that all three initiatives are progressing well.

Financial Market Reforms

First, the proposal to demutualise and merge the exchanges and clearing houses is well on track. Following the approval by the members of the exchanges in September, the enabling legislation was introduced in the LegCo in November. We expect the Exchanges and Clearing Houses (Merger) Ordinance to be enacted by the Legislature in the coming months. It is a testimony of Hong Kong resilience that the merger had the overwhelming support of the securities community. There is no doubt in my mind that the merger was strategically important to Hong Kong maintaining its international status, which has resulted in more active discussions with international exchanges on alliances or greater cooperation. The SFC welcomes the initiatives of the HKEC in their international discussions, and will help pave the way through active discussion and cooperation with the related regulators.

Secondly, the Steering Committee on the Enhancement of Financial Infrastructure submitted its report to the Financial Secretary in October. The Report contains a blueprint for the Hong Kong securities and futures market to upgrade its financial infrastructure to face the challenges of the globalised international financial markets of the new millennium. Work has already started to implement the blueprint. We have established a Steering Committee to implement the first step, the SDNet, with representatives from the exchanges, clearing houses and other market participants. This is an exciting step forward to improve the technology of the Hong Kong markets, which has become the real driver of market change.

Thirdly, drafting of the Securities and Futures Bill has reached a very advanced stage. The mammoth drafting task, which has taken at least five years of hard work and still counting, is nearing its completion. This owes much to the hard work and efforts of the Financial Services Bureau and the Department of Justice. We are particularly grateful for the input that we have received from market participants.

During the year, besides the above three initiatives, the work of the operating divisions of the Commission has been very busy. Most notably, we have prepared the regulatory groundwork for the successful launch of the GEM in November. This involved close liaison and cooperation with our Mainland counterpart – the Chinese Securities Regulatory Commission. The SFC has also completed the licensing regime review, introduced the draft margin financing legislation, issued a guidance note on internet trading and launched many other initiatives.

We have also been quite active to promote Hong Kong’s interest in the international front. As you are aware, we are active in IOSCO activities, as Andrew Procter is the Chairman of the IOSCO Task Force on Implementation of Core Principles. I was appointed in November to Chair the Financial Stability Forum Task Force on Implementation of Global Standards and in December was appointed to the IASC Nominating Committee to appoint Trustees for the International Accounting Standards Committee.

In the last year, I indicated that the work of the Commission, other than the major reform efforts, would concentrate on transparency and investor education. Let me say briefly what we have done in this area and what the Commission intends to achieve in the coming year.

Looking Ahead: Investors Beware

Looking ahead, there is no doubt in my mind that technology is the driving force of change in the New Millennium. This major trend change is reflected in the euphoria in investor interest in technology-related stocks that helped propel the stock market recovery that started in the last quarter of 1999. In the past quarter, the blue chips have gained 33%, while individual technology-related stocks have gained much more. More recently, the positive mood has transcended beyond first and second liners into third and fourth liners. A number of third and fourth liners have transformed themselves also into internet or high-tech concept companies.

The internet era has changed the whole ball game, where traditional wisdom may no longer apply and the rules of the game yet to be established. Evaluation and valuation of high-tech concept becomes very complex. Everyone wants to get into this new field and there will be winners as well as losers. It is not the regulators’ job to stop investors making money through speculation. Regulators do not have expertise in judging high-tech better than the market. We do not wish to spoil the party, but it is our job to point out the risks and to make sure that everyone plays by the rules.

First of all, on investor protection, let me quote Arthur Levitt, Chairman of the US Securities and Exchanges Commission. He said that "investor protection – at its most basic and effective level – starts with the investor". From the investor point of view, it is now clear that investors must judge for themselves the risk before they invest. Whether the investment is in internet stocks, high-tech stocks, or indeed property or banking stocks, the environment has changed and investors must for their own interest evaluate their own risk appetite and try to assess the risks of their investment.

Ultimately, the investor must take responsibility for his or her own profits or losses. They must understand the consequences of rumour chasing. Share prices can go up as well as down.

In my mind, the three golden rules for all investors are –

1. know what you are buying;
2. know the ground rules under which you buy and sell a stock or bond; and
3. know the level of risk you are undertaking.


We do not and cannot tell investors what or when to invest, but we will be doing our best to provide better investor education, so that they can have more information to make their own decisions. In the coming year, one of our main investor education efforts would be the establishment of the eIRC, or Electronic Investor Resources Centre. We seek to provide a one-stop reference centre for investors who can access its service without the confines of a physical centre by operating 24 hours a day and 365 days a year, or rather 366 in year 2000. It seeks to provide investors with the right information and links to enable them to decide and assess their investment portfolios. This will be linked to the exchanges and clearing house websites, so that the investor can readily access information. 

However, investors rely on the issuers, the investment advisers and the media to provide them with the information to make their decisions. It is this area where we are working with the securities and issuing community to disclose as much as possible and as timely as possible for the market to assess risks and returns.

We have improved the disclosure standards of listing companies quite considerably in recent years. We are continually working with the exchanges and the accountants on areas such as the SEHK Listing Rules and GEM Listing Rules to bring us up to best international standards, befitting our status as an international financial centre.

We have put these policy intentions into the new draft Securities and Futures Bill. As you are aware, amongst the Government’s 30 point programme is the criminalization of false reporting to the regulators. We believe that this is very important in the context of developing markets, especially in the high-tech and growth enterprise areas.

Make no mistake. We are fully in support of technology, innovation and growth enterprises. But public capital raising comes at a price and responsibility. The issuers and the promoters should disclose material information accurately, fairly and on a timely basis to the market. I have always maintained that the quality of a securities market does not depend on the quality of regulation alone. It depends first and foremost on the quality of the companies. Investors will come if they trust and understand the quality and integrity of the management of the companies they invest in. No one will invest in any market if they believe that the market is manipulated, especially by false or misleading information.

An example is the disclosure of the "hit rate" in internet websites. Many investors are looking at hit rates rather than past profitability to value an internet stock. However, at this stage, the reliability of the reported hit rates and the audit arrangements are yet to be well established. I think internet companies should have some independent verification of their disclosed "hit rate" and internet membership, so that the market will have more reliable information to assess their progress.

The SFC has already joined the Internet Task Force of IOSCO to tap international expertise in monitoring and regulating the development of internet on the securities market. As a regulator, we will work very closely with the HKEC and the securities community to improve the quality of disclosure. I hope the media, which has great influence in investor education, will also help to play its role.

As I said, as a regulator we have to do tasks, which are sometimes described as "taking away the punchbowl while the party gets going". We have been, and we will be continuously looking closely into any unusual market activities associated with stocks that exhibit sharp and sudden price movements. So do not be surprised if we say every now and again that we are watching closely for market manipulation and other misconduct that jeopardise the interests of the investors.

Transparency and Accountability

For the SFC itself, I set for ourselves higher standards of transparency and accountability. In the past year, we have issued eight guides and codes, five consultation documents, over 150 press releases and five other reports and documents to account for our work, introduce, explain and solicit views on our new initiatives and proposals. And in the past nine months, we have spent over 1,300 man-hours of preparation and actual attendance time to introduce and explain our legislative and other proposals to the public through attending meetings of LegCo panel, committees and sub-committees and responding to various enquiries from our Legislators.

As promised, we have also increased our dialogue with the market participants, including our registrants. In 1999, we have stepped up our dialogue with the Hong Kong Stockbrokers Association, the Securities Institute and key market players. We have established an Academic Advisory Committee, an Investor Education Advisory Committee and also draw in experts from the industry for our work on SCEFI and the legislation. Staff from our Enforcement Division, Intermediary Supervision Department and other units have held various formal and informal dialogues, regular and ad hoc meetings with the industry and media to draw in market wisdom and perceptions before we change any rules or policies.

These have enabled us to better explain the Commission’s position to the industry and also allowed us to gain a better understanding of our intermediaries’ and market views. I believe the benefits of increased dialogue are mutually felt and in the coming year I intend to intensify these dialogues.

Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing Limited

Besides all the important initiatives that I have alluded to earlier, perhaps the main focus in the coming two months would be on the establishment of the Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing Limited. This is a new chapter for the history of the securities and futures market in Hong Kong and would propel Hong Kong into a new era. The Commission looks forward to working closely with HKEC. Just as we have in the past year facilitated the launch of the GEM and the dual listing of Nasdaq stocks in Hong Kong, we stand ready to assist HKEC in its market development initiatives and its discussion with overseas counterparts on alliances and cooperation and other strategic moves.

The year 2000 is a year of opportunities and the SFC would continue to work with the exchanges, clearing houses and other market participants to make it a prosperous and successful year.

At this point of time, I want to thank my colleagues and our colleagues in the Administration who have worked so hard on so many issues during the last year. The most prominent example, other than the three major reforms, is the Y2K work. The regulator is most successful when nothing happens. Nothing happened in Y2K. The work that we did will be beneficial for us not only for the year 2000, but the lessons of cooperation, hardware and software review and investments made, will prove beneficial to the market for years to come.

I would be happy to answer any questions you may have after I have repeated some of the major points in English.

Happy New Millennium!
