“Technology may have created the New Economy, but it has not changed the fundamentals of the securities market,” said the Chairman of the Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) Mr Andrew Sheng at an investor education seminar to the Hong Kong Securities Institute today.

Technology has unleashed a potent combination of demand for capital by the new entrepreneurs of the New Economy, and a large supply of investors wishing to participate in this social and economic revolution. Technology has outstripped the capacity of many societies, including the regulatory framework to cope with rapid change. With more complex technology and knowledge, come greater opportunities and higher risks.

The first responsibility of investors is to take proper steps to protect themselves. The Commission accepts that it has a responsibility for investor protection and education, but we must work in partnership with the market.

In his speech, he elaborated on the risks and responsibilities being borne by all market participants, namely the investors, issuers, intermediaries and market professionals and the regulators (exchanges and SFC).

He reminded investors that when they are buying shares in companies without positive earnings, they are buying into concepts. In many cases, the concepts may not succeed and the earnings may never flow. Buying into the New Economy is no different from buying in any stocks, under three basic rules:

- Do you know what you are buying?
- Do you know the rules of the game under which you buy and sell?
- Do you know the level of risk that you are undertaking?
With the forthcoming implementation of on-line trading in Hong Kong, which has shown explosive growth elsewhere, there is a tremendous amount of investor education to be done to explain the benefits as well as the risks to the investors.

“For the issuers, raising public money carries public responsibilities. If things do not work out despite a lot of honest, hard work, then no one could be blamed. But if the controlling shareholders and management abuse the trust placed on them, misuse the capital given to them to turn concepts into real businesses and instead divert or squander it for private benefit, then they have wronged their investors. This will not be tolerated,” said Mr Sheng.

The investors also rely on sponsors, intermediaries and market professionals for their advice and judgement. They form part of the trust relationship with investors too. They have a huge role in explaining the rules of the game and the risks to the investors. They play the important role of responsible information analysis, and proper disclosure and dissemination. They are also charged with the duty of due care and trust, and should perform their jobs ethically, diligently, and properly.

On the regulatory framework for the New Economy, both the Stock Exchange and the SFC have become more and more aware that the remains of merit-based regulation in our system must give way to fully disclosure-based rules, especially in the GEM Board, he said.

Several key components are essential to the building of adequate investor protection. First, there must be full, accurate and timely disclosure. Second, through further investor education, investors must be empowered to understand the nature and risks of their investments and how to protect their rights. Thirdly, there must be adequate measures within the legal framework that would deter market misconduct and crime.

Many assume that if we were to replicate the success of NASDAQ, we would need simply to adopt the same regulatory framework or, if we did not adopt NASDAQ-type rules, Hong Kong would not be in a position to compete. We should understand that there are important differences between the Hong Kong and US systems, especially in the areas of enforcement tools and minority shareholder protection. The US regulatory regime carries significant fines. Disclosure of false or misleading information to a regulator is a criminal offence. Moreover, their legal system allows private class action, with potential for monetary awards at three times the actual damages suffered. These potentially financially crippling lawsuits, made easy and accessible to minority shareholders by contingency legal fee arrangements, are a critical link in the US approach to deterring poor disclosure, bad management and misleading information.

In Hong Kong, our enforcement tools and regulatory framework are still under debate and evolving. Hence, the risks for the uninformed and uneducated investors are higher.

This explains why there is a proposed amendment to the Securities Ordinance to make giving false and misleading information to regulators a criminal offence. It also explains why the GEM market insists that investors sign risk statements when they invest in this market.

“Our role is to maintain a level playing field and to regulate the trust relationships between investors, issuers and intermediaries. We will continue to conduct surveillance for manipulative activities or unfair trading. We will push for more full and timely disclosure by issuers. If an issuer or its management abuses the trust investors have placed on them, diverting capital from efforts to materialize concepts and to deliver on promises for private benefit, we will be there with all the legal and enforcement powers available to us.”

“The potential of the New Economy is huge and Hong Kong should be in the forefront of that growth. The Commission will help to facilitate and support that growth, which will bring opportunities and jobs for our citizens. But every society has to balance the need to innovate and the need to regulate. The regulators cannot prevent every accident or every financial crime. In a society governed by the rule of law, regulators can only regulate according to the powers and resources given to them by the people. There are limits to what regulators can do.”

“Everyone likes to join a party, but no one likes to clean up the mess. The best party is when everyone enjoys themselves and knows their limits. When you invest in the New Economy, understand the risks and know your limits”.
