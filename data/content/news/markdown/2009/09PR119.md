As market sentiment improves, more and more investors may be tempted to try and make a quick profit from initial public offerings (IPOs), without realising that not all new stocks rise above their offer price on debut.

Using historical data, Dr Wise explains in his latest column entitled “[IPOs: Is it really a quick profit?](http://www.invested.hk/invested/en/html/section/dr_wise/2009articles/ipo_aug09.html)” that some IPO shares perform worse than others, even if they get listed under the same market conditions. While it is intuitively obvious that IPO shares fare better when the market is on an uptrend, and vice versa there are a number of other factors that can come into play.

Firstly, new stocks in industries with a positive sector outlook tend to get more investor support and stand a better chance to rise in market value on listing.

A company’s own financial condition, its direction, its management vision and other fundamentals will also increase or decrease its appeal among investors.

Lastly, an IPO with a high trailing price-earning ratio may mean limited upside or potential gain. Investors should consider an IPO’s pricing in the context of listed companies in the same industry and not just the market in general, in order to make a more informed decision.

When making an IPO subscription, investors need to take into account transaction costs as well, especially if financing is used to cover the subscription costs. A few percentage points increase in a new stock’s price on its first trading day may not actually make you any profit.

Dr Wise also reminds investors that with hotly pursued IPOs, they may be allotted nothing or just one board lot. “You may end up holding onto a small stake that has little or no significance in your overall stock portfolio and holding it for a longer period than you want.”
